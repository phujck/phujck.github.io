EigenEngine

Eigen is a routing and permission system for AI agents, the programs that answer questions and carry out tasks over an organisation's documents. It stores every passage once, builds a map over the store, routes each question down the map to the three or four passages it needs, enforces who may read what, and returns every answer with its sources. The problem

What goes wrong with agents today

It reads everything, every time

An agent starts every question from nothing, so it reads every document again, and next week there are more of them. When one agent's answer feeds the next, a wrong source at the first step is carried into every step after it.

Nobody can check the answer

To find anything, an agent has to read everything. A question about a supplier reads the personnel files too, because there is no way to know a file is irrelevant, or private, without opening it. Nothing records which files were read, so nobody can check the answer against them.

How Eigen works

A routing and permission system on a recursive graph

Eigen reads the documents once and stores every passage. Over the store it builds a map, a graph: each passage is linked to the passages it shares a name, a date or a subject with; linked passages form a group; groups link to groups. Zoom into any group and you find the same shape again, passages linked to passages, which is what makes the graph recursive. A question is matched against the top of the map, then against the groups underneath, with permission checked at each step, and the answer comes back with the passages it used.

Persistence

Documents are read once and kept as a record of passages: what each says, the names, dates and numbers in it, and its links to other passages. The next question starts from the map instead of reading everything again.

Permission

An agent reaches the record only through the map, and opens only the groups its question needs. A file the agent may not read is never opened for it; if a fact it needs sits in one, Eigen delivers that fact alone, transformed as the rights policy for that agent requires, with a name or a figure removed. The owner of the documents sets the policy.

Provenance

Every answer carries the passages it used and a fingerprint of each. Anyone with the sources can check each passage themselves, and if a passage has changed since, the answer is flagged as no longer supported by its sources.

First public paper · November 2026

The cost of complexity

A physical theory of representation

Gerard McCaul, EigenEngine · 21 pages

Read the paper (PDF) Related talk: Synthetics arXiv listing to follow

This is the first paper produced with Eigen to be released publicly. Its formal derivations, figures and drafting were assisted by Eigen, which ran ChatGPT and Claude models. The author is responsible for the ideas, the interpretation and the correctness of the results.

The paper starts from the fact that anything that stores information is built from matter. A memory that holds one of sixteen values needs physical states to hold them, and it needs apparatus to select among them. The paper asks what that apparatus has to cost. It calls any physical system whose states stand for alternatives a register, and its argument rests on one observation: a register holds choices at one level and is itself one of the choices at the level above. A drawer in a filing cabinet works this way. It holds files, and it is itself one of the drawers to choose from. Four results follow.

Result 1 of 4

Organisation has a cost of its own

Sixteen symbols are numbered by rank, from the most used to the least used. They can sit in one register of sixteen states or be split across smaller registers. Splitting makes the choice inside each register smaller and adds a second choice: which register. The selector that makes this second choice is a register too, so it has to be paid for.

K(x) = C(Rx) + K(x | Rx)

The cost of reaching a symbol x is the cost of providing and selecting its register Rx, plus the cost of picking x out inside that register.

Result 2 of 4

Counting with physical registers costs a logarithm

The square is a register. Dividing it into four gives four registers, and each of those can be divided again. After k rounds it distinguishes 4k alternatives. What a round costs depends on what dividing consumes.

If dividing a register costs in proportion to its area, every round costs the same, and the budget for N alternatives is

K(N) = K0 + κ log N

K0 is the cost of a register existing at all. κ is the price of one nat of information. A nat is the natural-logarithm unit, one bit is 0.69 nats, and every logarithm on this page is a natural logarithm. If dividing instead costs in proportion to the length of the partition drawn across the register, each round costs twice the one before. If the price falls faster than area, each round costs half the one before and the total levels off.

The paper then proves a general statement. Small registers can be reused as the digits of a larger number, as the ten-state wheels of an odometer are. When that is allowed, the cheapest design grows no faster than a logarithm, at a rate set by the best price per nat among the registers available.

Result 3 of 4

Erasing a wrong guess releases heat

The paper applies the theory to a deliberately simple model of a computer. It guesses an answer, tests it, and erases the guess if the test fails. Erasing is the step with an unavoidable physical price.

Landauer's principle, an established result in the physics of computing, says the heat Q released by an erasure is at least kBT for each nat of uncertainty removed. That uncertainty is the entropy H of the guesses being erased. kB is Boltzmann's constant and T is the temperature of the surroundings. The paper adds a second floor that needs only the order of the guesses:

⟨Q⟩ ≥ kBT · H ≥ kBT · ⟨log r⟩

Angle brackets mean an average. r is the rank of an erased guess when guesses are listed from most to least probable. The floor depends on how the guesses rank and on nothing about how they are written down: a guess stored as a long number costs no more to erase than one stored as a short number.

Result 4 of 4

A logarithmic price gives a power law in rank

Take the cost of reaching the symbol of rank r to be log r, the form result 2 gives for a single symbol in the ideal case. Suppose the only other thing known about a system is its average cost. Maximum entropy is the standard rule for this situation: among all distributions with that average, take the one that is otherwise as spread out as possible. It is a power law:

p(r) ∝ r−λ

The multiplier λ sets how strongly expensive symbols are avoided. At λ = 1 this is Zipf's law, the pattern that word frequencies follow in many languages. Power laws of this kind have been derived before from other starting points. This derivation needs only the logarithmic cost and the average, and it leaves one free number, λ.

The paper is a static account. It prices an organisation of information under stated assumptions and does not yet price changing one. Eigen's map of a document store has the shape the paper studies: groups of passages that are themselves selected as members of larger groups. The paper does not analyse that map.

Coda · a second paper in one pass

Physical origins of the Kalmár constant

Draft of 19 November 2026 · 8 pages · not yet reviewed

After the paper above was finished, it was given to Eigen with a prompt to apply it. The paper compares ways of organising a register and never charges for choosing among them. Eigen priced that choice in a single pass, running a Claude model. The derivations, numerical checks, figures and text of this draft all came from that pass.

Read the draft (PDF)

Number theory already counts the ways to organise a register

Sixteen ranked symbols can be arranged in registers in eight ways, one for each way of writing 16 as an ordered product of whole numbers. For N symbols the count is the number of ordered factorisations of N. László Kalmár showed in 1931 that its growth is governed by a constant ρ = 1.7286…, the value of s at which the Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + … equals 2.

The draft reads Kalmár's constant as a capacity

If registers cost nothing to exist, each extra nat of budget multiplies the number of symbols a register can hold by e, and the number of ways to organise them by eρ. Give every register the existence cost K0 of result 2 and the exponent falls below ρ, along the curve shown. It approaches 1, the value of λ at which the Zipf family of result 4 can no longer be normalised for an unlimited vocabulary. The limit on how often symbols can be used in the first paper is therefore also the limit on how many ways expensive registers can be organised.

4 → 10binary digits

The draft also prices a register that stores the choice of its own organisation, then the organisation of that record, until nothing is left unrecorded. A binary register of k digits needs k(k + 1)/2 digits to do this. For sixteen symbols that is ten binary digits; four are enough for the symbols alone.

The number theory in the draft is classical. Its reading of ρ as a capacity, the curve above and the results on registers that record their own organisation are stated as new: the draft reports that a literature search did not find them and that the search was not exhaustive. It also predicts how self-organising hierarchies should branch, and says this prediction has not been tested against data. Its results are for registers whose sub-registers all have the same size, and they give the cost of holding an organisation, with nothing yet on the cost of changing it. The record does not always close: of the registers holding 2 to 10,000 symbols, the draft finds 817 that never finish recording themselves.

The draft's acknowledgements record who did what. They name Claude, the model Eigen was running. The results on capacity, on the ensemble of organisations and on self-recording registers were derived by the model at the author's prompting. The final hierarchy was proposed by the author and formalised by the model. The question the draft answers is one the first paper leaves open, and Eigen answered it in one pass.
