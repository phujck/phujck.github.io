EigenEngine

Eigen is software that sits between an organisation's documents and the AI agents that work on them. Agents read through Eigen instead of reading the documents directly. Eigen records what each agent read, controls which documents it may open, and returns the passages each answer was built from. Pre-release. eigen@gmccaul.co.uk

How it works

One record, read through an index, with every answer traced

Persistence

Documents are stored once, as a record of passages: what each says, the names, dates and numbers in it, and its links to other passages. The next question starts from the record instead of reading everything again.

Permission

An agent reaches the record only through the index, and opens only the branches its question needs. Branches it is not permitted to read are never opened.

Provenance

Every answer carries the passages it used and a fingerprint of each. Anyone with the sources can follow the chain back, and if a passage has changed since, the answer is flagged as no longer backed.

Use

Who it is for

Who

Companies whose AI products answer questions for lawyers, analysts, underwriters, doctors or researchers, where a wrong or unsupported answer is costly. Research groups are the first users.

How it is delivered

As software the customer runs inside their own systems. The documents never leave; only the text of each request goes to the customer's own AI provider.

Who sets the rules

The owner of the documents decides, document by document, which agents may read what. Eigen enforces it on every request.

Compared with today's tools

Today an AI's answer is judged by another AI, which gives a score. Eigen gives a record that the customer, or their auditor, can re-run themselves.

When

The first pilots are planned for early 2027.

Early prototypes

Eigen as part of an integrated research environment

The first use of Eigen has been as the record underneath a research environment: it holds a project's sources, plans and drafts, and papers and talks are written from it. Benchmark

SuperARC

A published test gives a model one hundred short sequences of ones and zeros and asks for the rule behind each. Eigen ran every proposed rule before answering with it. 96 of its 100 answers were real rules; most of the models in the published results wrote the sequence back out.
