Nonlinear Computing with Optical Twinning Trees

A prompt about experimentally realising twinning fields became a numerical optics paper about a feedback loop that finds zeros—and a composition rule that factors higher-order equations into matter.

Denys I. Bondar · Gerard McCaul, EigenEngine · with Eigen

The scientific idea

What is an optical twinning tree?

A twinning field is one electromagnetic drive Φ that makes two distinct materials produce the same chosen optical response: J₁[Φ] = J₂[Φ]. In the original construction, models of both materials are used to calculate that field in advance. The resulting pulse is stored and then replayed open loop.

This paper asks whether the materials can acquire the field themselves. A balanced detector measures only their response difference, e(t)=J₁(t)−J₂(t), and a delayed integral controller updates the same field that drives both samples. When the difference vanishes, the update stops. The two materials have physically evaluated a nonlinear residual, and the field held by the loop is its root.

The construction then becomes programmable. A Kerr cavity and a distinct linear cavity can be chosen so that their equality condition has the roots of a supplied quadratic. Two such Kerr leaves can share one linear reference; multiplying their measured residuals makes the combined signal vanish at the roots of either factor. That shared-reference composition is the optical twinning tree demonstrated numerically here.

The numerical test

Four questions the calculations answer

Will latency break the lock?

Yes, and the threshold is predictable. The scalar loop changes stability at κτ = 1.5675, within 0.0033 of the analytic boundary π/2. A practical finite-time lock requires the slightly smaller value κτ ≤ 1.53.

Does it survive interacting matter?

In the small exact test, yes. Two four-site Hubbard rings with different interaction strengths acquire one shared field. Their tail RMS current difference falls to 0.01194 at a gain–delay value just below the scalar boundary.

Can response equality encode an equation?

A Kerr–linear cavity pair is chosen so that matching intensities is equivalent to n² − 4n + 3 = 0. Fifteen of twenty-two tested initial conditions acquire one of its roots; the others reach the imposed optical power limit rather than a false solution.

Can two root conditions compose?

The three-cavity tree represents (s−1)(s−2)(s−3)(s−4) = 0. It acquires all four roots: thirty-three of forty-two runs reach a root, while the remaining nine end at declared power limits. None wander to an unclassified state.
