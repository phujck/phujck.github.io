Nonlinear Computing with Optical Twinning Trees

A prompt about experimentally realising twinning fields became a numerical optics paper about a feedback loop that finds zeros, and a composition rule that factors higher-order equations into matter.

The scientific idea

What is an optical twinning tree?

A twinning field is one electromagnetic drive Φ that makes two distinct materials produce the same chosen optical response: J₁[Φ] = J₂[Φ]. In the original construction, models of both materials are used to calculate that field in advance. The resulting pulse is stored and then replayed open loop.

This paper asks whether the materials can acquire the field themselves. A balanced detector measures only their response difference, e(t)=J₁(t)−J₂(t), and a delayed integral controller updates the same field that drives both samples. When the difference vanishes, the update stops. The two materials have physically evaluated a nonlinear residual, and the field held by the loop is its root.

The construction then becomes programmable. A Kerr cavity and a distinct linear cavity can be chosen so that their equality condition has the roots of a supplied quadratic. Two such Kerr leaves can share one linear reference. Multiplying their measured residuals makes the combined signal vanish at the roots of either factor. That shared-reference composition is the optical twinning tree demonstrated numerically here.

1 · TwinOne field drives two distinct materials to the same chosen response.
2 · ZeroThe measured response difference becomes the residual a feedback loop drives to zero.
3 · EncodeA Kerr-linear cavity pair makes that equality occur at the roots of a supplied quadratic.
4 · ComposeSeveral quadratic residuals share a reference. Three leaves recover six supplied roots.

The numerical tests

Four places where the physical picture could have failed

Is the response slope enough to predict stability?

No. It works only when the cavities settle much faster than the controller. The revised calculation keeps the cavity amplitudes inside the feedback dynamics and exposes the oscillatory instability that the reduced model misses.

What does the many-body loop actually follow?

The target moves because the material states move. Exact propagation of two four-site Hubbard rings confirms that integral feedback follows with a finite lag, while a state-informed feed-forward comparison shows what extra knowledge would be needed to remove it.

Does another branch simply give more roots?

Algebraically, yes. Dynamically, no. Three quadratic leaves recover six supplied roots across separate runs, but the slowest and fastest local convergence rates differ by more than two orders of magnitude. Depth has a conditioning cost.

Does a good lock guarantee the right answer?

No. Noise and delay can destroy acquisition, while cavity-parameter error poses a subtler danger: the loop may still reach equal intensities after the encoded polynomial has shifted. The revised robustness map reports optical locking and intended-root recovery separately.
