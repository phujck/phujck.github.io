The Cost of Complexity: A Physical Theory of Representation
Every representation must be embodied in matter, and this embodiment carries a cost. The cost extends beyond the symbols being manipulated to the structure of the registers that hold them, so the same distinctions can be prohibitively expensive in one organisation and accessible in another. We ask what constrains this freedom when the apparatus that organises a representation must itself be represented, and make three contributions. First, we show that efficiency and recursive self-consistency alone fix the form of representational cost: under additive positional composition the efficient register budget grows logarithmically, symbol costs can approach the same rate, and maximum entropy at fixed mean cost yields the Zipf family of distributions. Second, we establish a correspondence between this cost and the geometry of space-filling curves, in which the exponent of a refinement law is set by the dimension of the resource each division consumes and can be read from how often a small perturbation changes a register’s address. Third, we realise the cost thermodynamically in a Diophantine guessing machine, a minimal model of general computation whose heat of erasure is bounded below by the mean logarithm of probability rank. Together these results trace the logarithmic structure common to information theory, thermodynamics and Zipf’s law to a single origin in the efficient embodiment of distinctions. They offer a foundation for a covering theory across these fields, in which the organisation of a representation is itself a physical object of optimisation.
