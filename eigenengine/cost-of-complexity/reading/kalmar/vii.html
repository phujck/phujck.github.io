VII. The reflective hierarchy

The organisation tower prices the organisation of each register in it. It leaves one thing fixed: how the registers of the tower are themselves grouped. We now price that, and what the result leaves fixed in turn. We work with binary registers and count budgets in digits.

Let A₁(k) = k be the budget of a register of k digits whose organisation is supplied from outside. By Proposition 4, the closed tower obeys

A2(k)=A1(k)+A2(k−1),A2(1)=1,A_2(k)=A_1(k)+A_2(k-1),\qquad A_2(1)=1, (29)

since the organisation of k digits is a composition of k, held by k − 1 binary marks in a register that is closed in the same way. The closed tower on k digits has k constituents, the registers of the tower. Their grouping into consecutive blocks is again a composition of k, specified by k − 1 marks. A level-three object holds this choice in a register that is itself closed at level three, so A₃(k) = A₂(k) + A₃(k − 1). An object of level n on k digits has k constituents of level n − 1, on k, k − 1, …, 1 digits, and the same step applies.

Definition 3. The reflective hierarchy is the family of budgets defined by

An(k)=An−1(k)+An(k−1),An(1)=1,A_n(k)=A_{n-1}(k)+A_n(k-1),\qquad A_n(1)=1, (30)

for n ≥ 1, with A₀(k) = 1.

The step from level two to level three is a modelling choice. It prices one freedom left by the level below, the grouping of constituents. Other freedoms could be priced instead. The results that follow hold for the recursion (30).

Theorem 4 (Reflective hierarchy). Let N = 2k.

1. The closure cost at level n is

An(k)=(k+n−1n)=(log2N)nn![1+O(1/k)].A_n(k)=\binom{k+n-1}{n}=\frac{(\log_2 N)^n}{n!}\bigl[1+O(1/k)\bigr]. (31)

2. Depth and content are exchangeable: Aₙ(k + 1) = Aₖ(n + 1).

3. The functions A₀, A₁, A₂, … form a basis over the integers for the integer-valued polynomials in k.

4. For |x| < 1,

∑n≥0An(k)xn=(1−x)−k=Nα,α=log211−x.\sum_{n\ge0}A_n(k)x^n=(1-x)^{-k}=N^\alpha,\qquad\alpha=\log_2\frac{1}{1-x}. (32)

Part (i) shows that each reflective level adds one power of ln N. Part (iii) states that every budget which is an integer-valued polynomial in the number of digits is a unique integer combination of the reflective costs. Part (iv) resums the hierarchy. If the reflective depth is geometric, P(n) = (1−x)xn, the mean budget is (1−x)Nα, a power law in the range.

Equation (32) connects the two cost families of Ref. [1]. The scale-covariant budgets found there have excess gα(N) = κα(Nα − 1)/α, the q-logarithm with q = 1 − α [17]. By Eq. (32),

Nα−1α=1α∑n≥1An(log2N)xn,\frac{N^\alpha-1}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{n\ge1}A_n(\log_2N)x^n, (33)

so the q-logarithm is the generating function of the reflective closure costs. As x → 0 only the first level contributes and the right-hand side tends to ln N. At x = 1/2, α = 1 and

∑n≥0An(k)2−n=2k=N,\sum_{n\ge0}A_n(k)2^{-n}=2^k=N, (34)

the budget law of the unary register, with one element per distinction [Fig. 3(b)].

Remark. Aₙ(k) is the number of multisets of size n drawn from k types, equivalently the number of ways to distribute n indistinguishable quanta over k modes. Equation (32) is then the grand partition function of k bosonic modes at fugacity x. We record this as a formal correspondence.

Two limits should be kept apart. At fixed content the cost has no limit in the depth: by part (ii), Aₙ(k) ∼ nk−1 /(k − 1)! as n → ∞. The weighted sum does have a limit, and it is a power law. Reference [1] shows that a power-law budget is undercut by positional composition whenever that composition is available. The resummed hierarchy is therefore simple to describe, with a single exponent, and expensive to realise. Reflection on its own organisation does not reduce a register’s budget.
