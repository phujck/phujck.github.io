The Cost of Complexity: A Physical Theory of Representation · Gerard McCaul

V. The Canonical Cost Curve

So far we have asked what it costs to support distinctions and what is paid when uncertainty over them is erased. A register’s use also has statistics: some symbols are encountered often, while others are rare. The cost law does not by itself specify those frequencies. We must distinguish the physical constraint from the inference we make when that constraint is all we know.

We use the canonical symbol cost K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r, selected by homogeneous refinement and the prefix-budget idealisation of Eq. (31). Physical use induces a distribution p(r)p(r) over these states, with mean cost

ℓ=∑rp(r)logr.\ell=\sum_r p(r)\log r. (43)

This is the rank surprisal, the expected logarithmic cost of the occupied symbol. Many distributions share the same mean. Choosing one of them therefore requires an inference rule. If the budget is all we know, how do we avoid supplying additional structure merely because we prefer it?

The rank ensemble

The answer is supplied by Jaynes’ principle of maximum entropy [53]. Among distributions consistent with the stated constraints, it chooses the one that makes the least additional commitment relative to the specified state space and reference measure. This is the sense in which maximum entropy is a requirement of honest inference. The consistency requirements studied by Shore and Johnson provide a related axiomatic basis [54].

Here the reference is counting measure on the ranks: before conditioning on cost, each available rank receives the same weight. A different density of available states would instead enter as a prior weight. With this choice made, we maximise the Shannon entropy of a vocabulary of size RR,

S[p]=−∑r=1Rp(r)logp(r)S[p]=-\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log p(r) (44)

under

∑r=1Rp(r)=1,∑r=1Rp(r)logr=ℓ.\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)=1,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log r=\ell. (45)

The multiplier λ\lambda conjugate to mean cost prices departures towards more expensive ranks. The maximum-entropy weight of a state is exponential in its cost. Since that cost is logarithmic, e−λlogr=r−λe^{-\lambda\log r}=r^{-\lambda}: the exponential in cost becomes a power law in rank. Normalisation gives

p(r∣λ)=r−λZR(λ),p(r\mid\lambda)=\frac{r^{-\lambda}}{Z_R(\lambda)}, (46)

where

ZR(λ)=∑r=1Rr−λ.Z_R(\lambda)=\sum_{r=1}^R r^{-\lambda}. (47)

Theorem 5 (Zipf equilibrium).

Let R≥2R\ge2 and K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r on {1,…,R}\{1,\dots,R\}. For 0<ℓ<logR0<\ell<\log R, the maximum-entropy distribution at fixed mean cost ℓ\ell is p(r∣λ)=r−λ/ZR(λ)p(r\mid\lambda)=r^{-\lambda}/Z_R(\lambda). The equation of state ℓ(λ)=−∂λlogZR(λ)\ell(\lambda)=-\partial_\lambda\log Z_R(\lambda) uniquely determines finite λ\lambda. The endpoint ensembles are obtained as λ→±∞\lambda\to\pm\infty.

The theorem says that a power law need not be supplied as a further guess about the occupation statistics. It is the canonical maximum-entropy weight of a logarithmically priced rank. The calculation, together with its extension to scale-covariant costs, is given in Appendix G. For rank to agree with decreasing occupation probability we use λ≥0\lambda\ge0, with λ=0\lambda=0 giving uniform occupation.

Mandelbrot obtained related power laws by assigning logarithmic word costs [42]. The preceding register and symbol theorems explain where the leading rate of that cost can come from once the organisation is itself included. The canonical model goes further by taking the logarithmic symbol law to be exact. Under that cost and the stated mean constraint, the occupation law is then fixed rather than fitted independently. This is the sequence of the argument: composition constrains the cost, and maximum entropy turns that cost into a distribution. Moreover, this rank law is not tied to a single mechanism. Power law distributions have been derived in a variety of previous contexts  [55, 56]. Our contribution is to establish the minimal systemic properties under which these Zipfian distributions obtain. The fact that these describe so many biological and societal phenomena is extremely suggestive. Consider for example that word frequencies in many languages exhibit a power law distribution with exponents close to λ=1\lambda=1. From this we may infer two facts. First, that natural language is close to an optimal representation under additive composition [Footnote 3. one could argue that language creation is a form of subadditive composition. No cap.] . Secondly, its exponent tells us that we humans and bots are prepared to pay an enormous cost for linguistic facility. Understanding why this last statement is true must be true is our next concern.

The cost curve

What does this distribution allow us to calculate? The partition sum is more than a normalising constant. Changing λ\lambda shifts weight between cheap and expensive ranks, and derivatives of its logarithm tell us how the mean cost responds. Substituting the same probabilities into the entropy gives a second quantity along that curve:

C(λ):=−ddλlogZR(λ)=ℓ(λ),C(\lambda):=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log Z_R(\lambda)=\ell(\lambda), (48)
S(λ)=λC(λ)+logZR(λ).S(\lambda)=\lambda C(\lambda)+\log Z_R(\lambda). (49)

For λ>0\lambda>0, the corresponding free complexity is

F(λ):=C(λ)−λ−1S(λ)=−λ−1logZR(λ).F(\lambda):=C(\lambda)-\lambda^{-1}S(\lambda) =-\lambda^{-1}\log Z_R(\lambda). (50)

The multiplier acts as an inverse complexity temperature: large λ\lambda concentrates occupation on cheap ranks, while smaller λ\lambda spreads it across the vocabulary. Free complexity packages this competition between cost and the breadth of occupation in the familiar canonical form. The statistical mechanics is already recognisable, although a physical bath temperature enters only when we specify an operation such as the reset of Section IV.

How does this change when the vocabulary has no fixed upper limit? Taking R→∞R\to\infty, the partition sum becomes

Z∞(λ)=∑r=1∞r−λ=ζ(λ),λ>1.Z_\infty(\lambda)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}r^{-\lambda} =\zeta(\lambda),\qquad\lambda>1. (51)

The canonical cost curve therefore leads, in the infinite-vocabulary limit, to the Riemann zeta function. It arrives here as a sum over physically priced ranks. The boundary λ=1\lambda=1 marks the point at which suppression of expensive states no longer compensates for the unbounded range of states available. No normalised infinite ensemble exists there.

A finite register has no such difficulty. At finite RR, λ=1\lambda=1 gives the harmonic Zipf distribution pr=1/(rHR)p_r=1/(rH_R), with HR=∑r=1R1/rH_R=\sum_{r=1}^R1/r. Each multiplicative interval of rank carries approximately the same probability away from the endpoints: the vocabulary spreads its occupation over scales rather than choosing one preferred scale. Its sensitivity to the cost multiplier is

χ(λ):=−dCdλ=Varλ(logr).\chi(\lambda):=-\frac{dC}{d\lambda} =\operatorname{Var}_{\lambda}(\log r). (52)

Figure 6 shows the family and its response functions. The infinite-vocabulary zeta pole gives C∼(λ−1)−1C\sim(\lambda-1)^{-1} and χ∼(λ−1)−2\chi\sim(\lambda-1)^{-2} as λ↓1\lambda\downarrow1. Finite vocabularies instead have finite responses, with CR(1)∼12lnRC_R(1)\sim\frac12\ln R and χR(1)∼112(lnR)2\chi_R(1)\sim\frac1{12}(\ln R)^2. Appendix H gives these limits.

When this is the ensemble undergoing the reset of Section IV, its mean heat obeys

⟨Qerase⟩≥kBT[λC(λ)+lnZR(λ)]≥kBTC(λ).\begin{aligned} \langle Q_{\mathrm{erase}}\rangle &\ge k_BT[\lambda C(\lambda)+\ln Z_R(\lambda)]\\ &\ge k_BT C(\lambda). \end{aligned} (53)

The divergence near the infinite-vocabulary boundary therefore has an operational cost per complete reset. Spreading occupation across more scales leaves more uncertainty to be removed when the memory is cleared. This gives the singularity a physical meaning without demanding that any finite device realise an infinite vocabulary. A device’s power budget also depends on how frequently it performs the reset.

The leading tail law extends beyond the exact logarithm. In units of κ∞\kappa_\infty, an organisation with f(r)=clnr+o(lnr)f(r)=c\ln r+o(\ln r) has normalisation threshold λc=1/c\lambda_c=1/c. Theorem 4 makes rates arbitrarily close to c=1c=1 achievable. A fixed organisation has the canonical threshold precisely when its leading rate is one. Lower-order coding overhead then decides what happens on the boundary, as illustrated by Rissanen’s universal integer prior [45].

A fixed weighting of the vocabulary can also change the tail. Write

pw(r∣λ)=w(r)e−λf(r)Zw(λ),Zw(λ)=∑r≥1w(r)e−λf(r).\begin{aligned} p_w(r\mid\lambda)&=\frac{w(r)e^{-\lambda f(r)}}{Z_w(\lambda)},\\ Z_w(\lambda)&=\sum_{r\ge1}w(r)e^{-\lambda f(r)}. \end{aligned} (54)

For f(r)=lnr+o(lnr)f(r)=\ln r+o(\ln r) and positive weights satisfying

lnw(r)=o(lnr),\ln w(r)=o(\ln r), (55)

the threshold remains one. The weights are r−λ+o(1)r^{-\lambda+o(1)}, so comparison with nearby powers establishes convergence for λ>1\lambda>1 and divergence for λ<1\lambda<1. At the boundary, w(r)=[ln(r+1)]−bw(r)=[\ln(r+1)]^{-b} with exact logarithmic cost gives normalisation for b>1b>1 and finite mean log-rank for b>2b>2. A power weight w(r)=r−aw(r)=r^{-a} instead shifts the threshold to 1−a1-a, while exponential suppression removes any finite normalisation boundary. Appendix I gives the comparison and the corresponding moment conditions.

The precise invariant is therefore the leading logarithmic tail scale, while homogeneous refinement gives the exact zeta family. Asymptotic logarithmic costs and subpower weights retain its threshold away from the boundary, but need not retain its critical response. Equation (54) is a conditional model, not a general solution of an encoder optimisation with a mutual information constraint. That optimisation also changes which states represent the target, and is the next question.

Preserving a target. But what makes a representation useful rather than merely cheap? A register that always returns the same symbol is economical, but tells us nothing about a target that varies. Nor does a broad occupation distribution establish usefulness: the variation may be unrelated to what we want to predict. The missing question is what the symbols preserve about YY.

To make that question explicit, let WW be a random source configuration taking values ω∈Ω\omega\in\Omega, fix the joint law p(ω,y)p(\omega,y), and vary an encoder q(x∣ω)q(x\mid\omega) over a specified admissible class. We also use XX and YY for the resulting random variables. The encoder gives the Markov chain X−W−YX-W-Y.

We use Iq(X;Y)I_q(X;Y) as fidelity because it measures the reduction in target uncertainty supplied by the code [33]. Changing the names of the symbols does not change that information. Changing which source configurations they group together can. Thus learning a representation changes more than its occupation statistics: it changes which distinctions become accessible. Retaining the fixed rank cost of the chosen design, maximising occupation entropy at prescribed mean cost and relevance gives

maxqHq(X),C[q]=𝔼q[lnr(X)]=ℓ,Iq(X;Y)≥I0.\begin{gathered} \max_q H_q(X),\\ C[q]=\mathbb E_q[\ln r(X)]=\ell,\qquad I_q(X;Y)\ge I_0. \end{gathered} (56)

Its Lagrange functional for minimisation is

𝒥[q]=λC[q]−Hq(X)−βIq(X;Y),β≥0.\mathcal J[q]=\lambda C[q]-H_q(X)-\beta I_q(X;Y), \qquad\beta\ge0. (57)

Cost prices the occupied ranks, entropy rewards the breadth of occupation, and relevance rewards what those states reveal about the target. With a normalisable fixed reference law ρλ(x)=e−λK(x)/Z(λ)\rho_\lambda(x)=e^{-\lambda K(x)}/Z(\lambda),

𝒥[q]=D(pq(X)‖ρλ)−lnZ(λ)−βIq(X;Y).\begin{aligned} \mathcal J[q]&=D(p_q(X)\Vert\rho_\lambda)-\ln Z(\lambda)\\ &\quad-\beta I_q(X;Y). \end{aligned} (58)

The relation to information bottleneck depends on the cost calibration across encoders [22]. For a deterministic candidate class satisfying

C[q]=aHq(X)+b,a>0,C[q]=aH_q(X)+b,\qquad a>0, (59)

with the same a,ba,b throughout, Hq(X)=Iq(W;X)H_q(X)=I_q(W;X). If λa>1\lambda a>1, a positive rescaling of 𝒥−λb\mathcal J-\lambda b then gives
