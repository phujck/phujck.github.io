Abstract

Every representation must be embodied in matter, and this embodiment carries a cost. This cost extends not only to the symbols being manipulated, but the structure of the registers which hold those symbols. Utilising the same set of distinctions can therefore be prohibitively expensive in one organisation and accessible in another. We ask what constrains this freedom of representation when the apparatus that organises a representation must itself be represented. Our central observation is recursive: a register holds distinctions at one level and is itself selected by a distinction at the next. With additive positional composition and a positive limiting price per nat, we derive logarithmic growth of the efficient register budget and show that symbol costs can approach the same asymptotic rate. We demonstrate this bound is universal, but the rate at which it can be approached depend on the particular representation. Homogeneous refinement makes the logarithmic law exact, while maximum entropy at fixed mean logarithmic rank cost yields the Zipf family of distributions. We then independently show that this result can be observed in a thermodynamic computer, where the mean log probability rank gives a lower bound on the heat of erasure. Requiring fidelity to a target extends the account to the information a representation must preserve. The resulting theory makes the physical organisation of information an object of optimisation, providing a static foundation for representation learning and systems that can revise their own architecture

I. Introduction

The most successful theories in science are organised around a balancing act. In classical mechanics, kinetic and potential terms enter the same action. In statistical mechanics, equilibrium reflects a balance between energy and entropy, while learning theory examines how model capacity shapes bias and variance [1, 2] and the trade-off between compression and fidelity [3]. The quantities being balanced belong to different ontologies, yet the recurrence of competing demands invites a common question: what does a representation have to carry, and what does it cost to carry it?

Any answer must account for the physical embodiment of information, as there exists no Platonic register upon which dynamics can act. Theories are used through substrates that store, manipulate and erase distinctions under finite resource budgets. A substrate-independent account must therefore describe those distinctions generally enough to connect different fields, while leaving each description its own interpretation and notion of efficiency.

This question has become harder to ignore in the present moment. Large-scale machine learning makes the resource constraints plain, motivating the search for more efficient representations of models [4, 5]. Practical success does not diminish the need for a theory of the process. Steam engines could be realised before thermodynamics, but their operation gave the theory something concrete to explain. History is now repeating itself in the age of information engines, and the approaching era of recursive self-improvement makes the need for a theoretical description particularly urgent. In both proof-guided and empirically evaluated approaches, the procedure performing the optimisation can itself be modified [6, 7, 8]. The physical account must therefore include the machinery that proposes, evaluates and retains revisions, as well as the representation being revised.

We address the static part of that account. Our starting point is a representation equipped with an order of typicality and a physical budget for realising its symbols. The additional observation is that a register has two roles: it holds distinctions at one level and is itself selected by a distinction at the next. This recursion brings the organisation of a representation into the same cost account as its contents. When registers can be composed positionally at additive cost, their efficient budget has a well-defined asymptotic price per nat. A positive price gives logarithmic growth, while homogeneous refinement makes the law exact.

Representation has long played this organising role in physics. Renormalisation preserves large-scale descriptions while discarding microscopic detail [9], and predictive-state constructions retain the distinctions needed for future prediction [10]. Influence functionals recast environmental effects as stochastic open-system dynamics [11, 12] and provide a route to mean-force Hamiltonians [13]. Other transformations relate imaginary-time evolution to ensembles of unitary dynamics [14, 15], map free to harmonic motion in quantum and Koopman dynamics [16], or expose quantum–classical correspondence through the waveoperator [17]. Each makes some structure accessible by changing how it is represented. The cost of choosing and supporting that representation is the concern of the present work.

Several literatures already orbit aspects of this problem. Landauer’s principle relates the entropy removed by reset to its heat cost [18, 19, 20], while efficient coding asks how signals can be organised economically into useful variables [21]. The information bottleneck makes utility explicit through a target whose information must be preserved [22]. Closer to physics, the thermodynamics of prediction connects predictive organisation to dissipation [23], and resource theories specify which transformations the available operations can achieve [24, 25]. Finally constructor theory formulates physical constraints in terms of possible and impossible transformations [26, 27]. Together these approaches make clear why a theory of representation needs to account for both the cost of distinctions represented, and the operations through which they become useful.

Section II introduces the representation, its target and the efficiency assumptions. In Section III we develop the recursive cost account, derive the register and symbol bounds, and give their geometric interpretation. Section IV connects probability rank to the heat of erasure, using Diophantine search to relate a guessing machine to general computation. Section V derives the conditional Zipf family and extends the cost functional to representations that preserve information about a target. We conclude in Section VI with the consequences for physical learning and self-modification. The individual proofs and technical extensions are collected in Appendices A–J.

II. Representations in Matter

Our starting point is deliberately sparse. A representation must be realised in some physical system, and its use draws on the resources of that system. This applies to the apparatus that organises the representation as much as to the symbols it holds [Footnote 1. There are no exceptions. It applies just as much to the theory proposed here as to any domain of application. This makes everything that follows unavoidably self-referential, and more than a bit meta.] . Thermodynamic accounts of computation make these resources explicit through the operations performed, their duration and the states available to implement them [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. We therefore seek a description of structural organisation before choosing a particular substrate.

To explore the consequences of embodiment, we must first establish what is being represented and what carries the representation. Let Ω\Omega be the space of microscopic configurations relevant to the problem, and let y:Ω→Yy:\Omega\to Y specify the target distinctions. The target need not be a complete microscopic description. It might ask which of sixteen intervals contains an instrument reading, without asking for the positions of every atom in the instrument.

We call a physical system a register when its distinguishable states are used to realise a set of alternatives. The term is not restricted to computer memory. The position of a pointer, the level of an electrical signal, or a pattern across several physical elements can all serve this purpose. A register may therefore be a single device or an organised collection of devices. Its range is the number of alternatives it can distinguish at the resolution required by the task.

Write RR for the physical register and XX for its set of symbols. These play different roles: RR is the apparatus, while x∈Xx\in X names one of the alternatives realised by that apparatus. Sixteen symbols need not require sixteen separate objects. They could be sixteen positions of one pointer or sixteen joint configurations of several elements. The readout specifies which physical configurations count as the same symbol. We express this through the symbol map

π:Ω→X.\pi:\Omega\longrightarrow X. (1)

The configurations in Ax=π−1(x)A_x=\pi^{-1}(x) all realise the same symbol. Microscopic changes within AxA_x leave the readout unchanged, so the symbol map treats those configurations as indistinguishable. This is the coarse-graining supplied by the representation. What matters is whether the distinctions it removes are needed for the target.

The representation is faithful to the target when π(ω)=π(ω′)\pi(\omega)=\pi(\omega') implies y(ω)=y(ω′)y(\omega)=y(\omega'). It may then discard microscopic detail while retaining everything the target requires. For a probabilistic target the corresponding condition is that its conditional distribution depend on ω\omega only through π(ω)\pi(\omega), the familiar sufficiency condition [33]. When the target is the distribution of future observations, this leads to the causal-state construction of computational mechanics [10].

Critically, the target may include the register itself. This is the situation faced by a system that represents its own organisation, and it requires the apparatus to remain inside the resource account. Exact fidelity is still a question about the chosen target: a one-bit register can preserve a binary property of a much larger system. When the target demands more distinctions than the available register can hold, the representation must choose what to retain.

Which distinctions can a physical device infer in the first place? This is answered within the inference-device framework, which treats observation, prediction and recollection within a common formal account [34]. We therefore take the target as given and ask what it costs to support the distinctions needed to represent it.

The choice of register is itself a distinction that must be physically represented however, as changing the organisation leaves the meaning of x11x_{11} intact, but changes how it is reached, and therefore the cost incurred. The price of embodiment

A symbol map tells us which distinction is represented, but not what must be provided to realise it. We therefore assign a realisation cost Kℛ(x)K_{\mathcal R}(x) to symbol xx in an organisation ℛ\mathcal R. Here ℛ\mathcal R specifies the arrangement of registers, the assignment of symbols to their states, and the means of selecting among them. A particular register RR is one component of that organisation. Changing ℛ\mathcal R can change the cost of reaching xx without changing what xx represents.

All comparisons use the same resource, operating conditions and task. Depending on the application, the budget may price apparatus, a prescribed operation, or a declared combination of them. Providing a register with sixteen possible states and repeatedly resetting one of those states are different resource questions. Experimental erasure and finite-time information processing illustrate why the operation must be specified when the budget is given an energetic meaning [35, 36, 37]. Under the standard reset assumptions, erasing an unbiased bit at temperature TT has a mean heat floor kBTln2k_BT\ln2. The general Landauer bound prices the entropy removed [18, 19, 20]. Section IV will use this operation to calibrate a logarithmic cost in energy units. For the time being however we will proceed with maximum generality, and use only the fact that physical manipulations are never free.

What makes one allocation of these costs more efficient than another? A representation is more than a set of labels. Its symbols stand in correspondence with the observations or alternatives they describe, and some may be encountered more readily than others. We begin with only this relative information, rather than a complete probability measure. Instead we shall rely only on the weaker notion of typicality,

x≽x′⇔x is at least as typical as x′.x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{$x$ is at least as typical as $x'$}. (2)

Typicality records which symbols are more prevalent or readily encountered. It says that one comes before another, without specifying how much more probable it is. If usage probabilities are known, they can supply this order, but the order alone does not determine them. We consider a finite vocabulary or a countable sequence in which every symbol has finitely many predecessors. For clarity we initially take distinct typicalities. The order can be represented by a scalar τ\tau, but changing its numerical scale leaves the ordering intact. Our argument must therefore depend on that order rather than on an arbitrary numerical encoding of it.

Our efficiency condition asks that cost respect this order:

x≽x′⇒Kℛ(x)≤Kℛ(x′).x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\mathcal R}(x)\le K_{\mathcal R}(x'). (3)

The motivation is the usual assignment argument. Suppose two physical states have different costs, and the symbol used more often has been assigned to the more expensive state. Exchanging their assignments reduces the mean cost. Repeating this comparison favours placing more probable symbols in cheaper states, whenever the physical implementation permits those exchanges [38, 39, 33].

Eq.(3) retains only the ordinal part of this argument. It permits equal costs and does not demand an optimal code. Nor does it specify how much a register costs when its range is enlarged or its contents are redistributed. Those questions concern the organisation of the representation. To answer them, we must include the cost of selecting a register alongside the cost of distinguishing its contents.

III. The Cost of Complexity

Typicality orders the distinctions a representation carries. We now ask how their costs depend on the registers through which they are reached. For a vocabulary with finite typicality prefixes, define

r(x):=#{x′∈X:x′≽x},r(x):=\#\{x'\in X:x'\succcurlyeq x\}, (4)

such that with distinct typicalities this is the ordinal position of xx. An order-preserving change of the typicality scale leaves it unchanged.

Observation (Rank gauge).

For an order-respecting representation on such a vocabulary, there is a nondecreasing function fℛf_{\mathcal R} such that

Kℛ(x)=fℛ(r(x)).K_{\mathcal R}(x)=f_{\mathcal R}\bigl(r(x)\bigr). (5)

Each rank identifies one symbol, so this is the cost function expressed in the invariant coordinate supplied by the order. It does not yet fix the function fℛf_{\mathcal R}. We must now ask how an efficient representation behaves under composition. It is tempting to assume additivity immediately, but that would miss the crucial insight upon which the rest of this work turns. That is, to account for the cost of providing and selecting the registers themselves.

There are two ways to accommodate more distinctions. We can enlarge the range of an existing register, or distribute the distinctions across additional registers. The latter makes the choice within each register smaller, but introduces another choice: which register contains the required symbol? If that choice is omitted from the account, every symbol could be given a register of its own and the internal cost would appear to disappear. At the other extreme, a single universal register makes selection trivial but must support all the distinctions internally. A meaningful comparison must account for both tasks.

The original problem therefore reappears at the level of the apparatus used to organise the symbols. To make this self-similarity explicit, take the first N=mnN=mn symbols in rank order and divide them into mm consecutive subregisters of nn symbols. The original rank can now be identified by the subregister index ii and the position jj within it:

r=n(i−1)+j,1≤i≤m,1≤j≤n.r=n(i-1)+j,\qquad 1\le i\le m,\quad1\le j\le n. (6)

This is a positional encoding, where one choice among mnmn alternatives has become a choice among mm registers followed by a choice among nn symbols. The two indices identify exactly the same distinction as the original rank, without requiring statistical independence between them. We illustrate this in Fig. 2 by returning to the sixteen symbols of Fig. 1. There, two registers of eight symbols and eight registers of two symbols both carry the same sixteen distinctions. The first index now exposes the recursion. At the level where registers are selected, ii is itself a symbol. Once that selection has been made, the chosen register supplies the space within which jj is distinguished. A register is therefore both the object selected by one distinction and the space supporting the next. Moving the boundary between these roles changes the organisation without changing which symbol is ultimately identified. Our problem is therefore self-similar, a fact which we can exploit.

We can now make the two contributions to the cost explicit. Fix a physical organisation ℛ\mathcal R and let RxR_x denote the register containing symbol xx. The complete cost includes both providing the apparatus through which RxR_x is selected and distinguishing xx once that register has been selected:

Kℛ(x)=Cℛ(Rx)+Kℛ(x∣Rx).K_{\mathcal R}(x) =C_{\mathcal R}(R_x)+K_{\mathcal R}(x\mid R_x). (7)

We call Cℛ(Rx)C_{\mathcal R}(R_x) the structural cost. It includes providing the apparatus and realising the choice of register, rather than merely charging a fixed amount for adding another box. The conditional contribution Kℛ(x∣Rx)K_{\mathcal R}(x\mid R_x) is the remaining cost of distinguishing xx within that structure. The vertical bar expresses this dependence on a selected register. No probability distribution is needed for the decomposition. It is important to note thatEq. (7) is an accounting identity, and does not rely on any assumption on the form of composition, nor a probabilistic model. The question of how representation compose is critical, as it determines how the two terms of cost change together. Moving symbols into a smaller register may make them cheaper to distinguish internally, but it also changes the selection through which they are reached. A saving in the first task is a saving overall only if it pays for the change in the second.

The limiting cases make this particularly clear. One universal register makes the selector trivial and leaves every distinction inside it. Giving each symbol its own register instead produces the one-to-one correspondence

x↔Rx.x\longleftrightarrow R_x. (8)

There is then no choice left within a selected register, but selecting that register requires precisely the original distinctions, with their original typicality order. The representational problem has moved into CℛC_{\mathcal R}. Neither limiting arrangement removes it. With the full account in place, we can ask when sharing a register reduces the cost and when separating its contents is preferable.

Recursive composition

A cost for one symbol and a budget for a whole vocabulary answer different questions. To support a range of NN alternatives, a register must be able to realise any one of them, including the most expensive. For a prescribed organisation ℛ\mathcal R, this requires the largest complete symbol cost on that range.

We also want to compare different organisations of the same distinctions. Let 𝒜N\mathcal A_N be the admissible organisations supporting the first NN symbols in the common typicality order, with all structural costs included. The choice of this class specifies which physical designs and compositions are available. We distinguish the budget of a particular design from the infimum over those alternatives:

Bℛ(N):=maxr(x)≤NKℛ(x),Kreg(N):=infℛ∈𝒜NBℛ(N).\begin{aligned} B_{\mathcal R}(N)&:=\max_{r(x)\le N}K_{\mathcal R}(x),\\ K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)&:=\inf_{\mathcal R\in\mathcal A_N}B_{\mathcal R}(N). \end{aligned} (9)

The maximum in BℛB_{\mathcal R} acts on the complete cost for the same symbol, including both terms of Eq. (7). It is a budget sufficient for any alternative in the vocabulary, not the sum of the costs of realising all alternatives simultaneously. The second quantity, KregK_{\mathrm{reg}}, is the efficient envelope of the available designs. Its infimum need not be attained by a single design [Footnote 2. In future work we shall use this to establish the theory’s connections to non-commutative theories such as quantum mechanics] .

We assume finite budgets and allow an organisation supporting MM symbols to serve any smaller prefix, retaining the full cost of its unused capacity. A sixteen-state register can therefore be used for its first ten alternatives without pretending that the other six states cost nothing to provide. This restriction property then lets us compare vocabularies of different sizes.

Theorem 1 (Monotonicity of the register budget).

Under this restriction property,

N≤M⇒Kreg(N)≤Kreg(M).N\le M\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)\le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(M). (10)

Any admissible organisation for MM is an available alternative for N≤MN\le M, at no larger maximum cost on that prefix. Taking the infimum over the alternatives proves the inequality. It is important to emphasise this inequality bounds complete budget given by Eq. (7), including both the register structural cost, and the cost of symbols within them.

We are now in a position to formulate the cost of a refinement. Refining each of mm alternatives into nn alternatives gives a total range of mnmn, not m+nm+n. That is, distinctions compose multiplicatively. Let K0=Kreg(1)K_0=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1) be the reference budget for a register with one alternative. We call this the existence cost K0K_0, as even when no internal choice remains, the apparatus can carry a cost. The additional budget for the refinement is the difference

K0:=Kreg(1),Δm(n):=Kreg(mn)−Kreg(m).K_0:=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1),\qquad \Delta_m(n):=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn)-K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m). (11)

These are differences of static budgets. Deferring the question of path dependence to a full dynamic treatment, we stipulate that budget difference depends only on the end points. Successive differences then telescope,

Δm(np)=Δm(n)+Δmn(p),\Delta_m(np)=\Delta_m(n)+\Delta_{mn}(p), (12)

without requiring a model of the work needed to rebuild the register. The price of a refinement can depend on the range already supported. We record that dependence through

ϵ(m,n):=Δm(n)−Δ1(n),\epsilon(m,n):=\Delta_m(n)-\Delta_1(n), (13)

which gives the general composition identity

Kreg(mn)=Kreg(m)+Kreg(n)−K0+ϵ(m,n).\begin{aligned} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn) &=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m)+K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)\\ &\quad-K_0+\epsilon(m,n). \end{aligned} (14)

The definition makes ϵ\epsilon symmetric, and consistent under successive composition:

ϵ(m,n)=ϵ(n,m),\epsilon(m,n)=\epsilon(n,m), (15)
ϵ(m,n)+ϵ(mn,p)=ϵ(n,p)+ϵ(m,np).\begin{aligned} &\epsilon(m,n)+\epsilon(mn,p)\\ &\qquad=\epsilon(n,p)+\epsilon(m,np). \end{aligned} (16)

These identities expose the possible corrections to additivity any self consistent representation may maintain. We first examine the case in which that correction vanishes, then ask what remains when the refinement price varies with scale.

Homogeneous refinement

What happens when the correction to multiplicative additivity vanishes? The simplest case is one in which the same relative refinement has the same price, whatever range the register already supports. In the notation just introduced,

Δm(n)=Δ1(n)for all m,n∈ℕ.\Delta_m(n)=\Delta_1(n)\qquad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb N. (17)

We call this homogeneous refinement. Each multiplication of the supported range has the same price, whatever the range already present. With

g(n):=Kreg(n)−K0,g(n):=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)-K_0, (18)

the composition identity becomes

g(mn)=g(m)+g(n).g(mn)=g(m)+g(n). (19)

Theorem 2 (Logarithmic register cost).

Let Kreg:ℕ→ℝ≥0K_{\mathrm{reg}}:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R_{\ge0} be finite and nondecreasing, and let its conditional increments satisfy Eq. (17). Then

Kreg(n)=K0+κlogn,K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)=K_0+\kappa\log n, (20)

where

κ=Kreg(2)−Kreg(1)log2≥0.\kappa = \frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(2)-K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1)} {\log 2} \ge0. (21)

Moreover, κ>0\kappa>0 if and only if Kreg(2)>Kreg(1)K_{\mathrm{reg}}(2)>K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1).

This result fixes the entire cost law, not only its large-range limit. Every doubling adds the same budget κlog2\kappa\log2, and every multiplication by nn adds κlogn\kappa\log n, whatever the range already supported. Monotonicity and homogeneous refinement suffice to determine that law without a probability measure or an assumed code length. The proof in Appendix A brackets nkn^k between powers of two to fix g(n)/logng(n)/\log n.

Does recursive consistency require refinement to be homogeneous? Not a chance. To see the additional freedom, fix a prescribed architecture with budget B(N)B(N) and define its increments by ΔmB(n)=B(mn)−B(m)\Delta^B_m(n)=B(mn)-B(m). Suppose the price of the same refinement changes with scale according to ΔqmB(n)=qαΔmB(n)\Delta^B_{qm}(n)=q^\alpha\Delta^B_m(n). For a nonzero refinement price, consistency under composition gives the scale-covariant family

B(N)=B(1)+{καNα−1α,α≠0,κ0logN,α=0.B(N)=B(1)+ \begin{cases} \displaystyle\kappa_\alpha\frac{N^\alpha-1}{\alpha}, &\alpha\ne0,\\[1ex] \kappa_0\log N,&\alpha=0. \end{cases}

The homogeneous logarithm is the α=0\alpha=0 member. Positive α\alpha gives power-law growth, while negative α\alpha gives a bounded excess budget. This is the qq-logarithmic family with q=1−αq=1-\alpha. Appendix D derives it and shows how the power-law scaling factor follows from a monotone dependence on scale ratios. This wider family prices a particular architecture, and does not yet bound efficient envelope over every architecture available. That distinction is decisive, as a representation can obey a perfectly consistent power law and still cost more than a positional construction assembled from its parts. This begs the question as to which of these cost laws can remain efficient once such alternatives are admitted? We now return to KregK_{\mathrm{reg}} to make that comparison.

Composition as shared cost

Suppose two admissible registers can implement the positional choice in Eq. (6) at the sum of their complete budgets. The infimum for mnmn distinctions cannot exceed that available construction, and applying the comparison to arbitrarily near-optimal registers gives

Kreg(mn)≤Kreg(m)+Kreg(n),K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn)\le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m)+K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n), (22)

If we then define the shared cost

IK(m:n):=Kreg(m)+Kreg(n)−Kreg(mn)=K0−ϵ(m,n)≥0.\begin{aligned} I_K(m{:}n)&:=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m)+K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)\\ &\quad-K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn)\\ &=K_0-\epsilon(m,n)\ge0. \end{aligned} (23)

we have a measure of the saving available when the two choices are organised together. Its form resembles mutual information, I(A;B)=H(A)+H(B)−H(A,B)I(A;B)=H(A)+H(B)-H(A,B) [33], and its symmetry and composition identity have the same algebraic form:

IK(m:n)+IK(mn:p)=IK(n:p)+IK(m:np).\begin{aligned} I_K(m{:}n)+I_K(mn{:}p) &=I_K(n{:}p)+I_K(m{:}np). \end{aligned} (24)

Here the quantities are budgets, and their non-negativity comes from the positional construction just specified. We emphasise again that the assumptions required to obtain this are pre-probabilistic, and rely solely on the demand for efficiency of representation. Even without assuming a particular cost law, much can be inferred from this relationship. Once positional composition is available at additive cost, a range need not be expanded by making one monolithic register ever larger. We can instead repeat a fixed digit of range bb. A string of kk such digits distinguishes bkb^k alternatives for a budget no greater than kKreg(b)kK_{\mathrm{reg}}(b). Supporting NN alternatives therefore requires at most ⌈logbN⌉\lceil\log_b N\rceil digits. This is the origin of the logarithmic ceiling: the range multiplies while the available budget adds. The next result shows that these comparisons also determine a limiting price per nat.

Theorem 3 (Asymptotic price of a distinction).

Let KregK_{\mathrm{reg}} be nondecreasing and satisfy Eq. (22) for all m,n∈ℕm,n\in\mathbb N. Then the limit

κ∞:=limN→∞Kreg(N)logN=infN≥2Kreg(N)logN\kappa_\infty := \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)}{\log N} = \inf_{N\ge2}\frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)}{\log N} (25)

exists, and for every N≥2N\ge2 and every integer b≥2b\ge2,

κ∞logN≤Kreg(N)≤Kreg(b)⌈logbN⌉.\kappa_\infty\log N \le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N) \le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(b)\,\lceil\log_b N\rceil. (26)

The theorem gives more than a logarithmic upper bound. The ratio Kreg(N)/logNK_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)/\log N converges to a single rate, and that rate is the infimum over all available register sizes. Any one digit of range bb supplies the upper bound in Eq. (26), while the lower bound says that no admissible organisation can undercut κ∞logN\kappa_\infty\log N. Together they identify the best asymptotic price of a distinction.

Why does the ceiling also determine a common limiting price? Repeated use of a fixed base turns multiplicative growth of range into an ordinary subadditive sequence of budgets, and the average price therefore converges to the infimum of its block prices. Monotonicity then lets powers of one base be bracketed between neighbouring powers of another, so every base gives the same limiting price per logarithmic range. A final bracket extends the result to arbitrary NN. Appendix B supplies the inequalities.

This is the generalisation beyond the homogeneous law of Theorem 2. The register need not have a logarithmic cost at every finite size, and no probability distribution has been introduced. Its implementation may have an uneven refinement price or expensive monolithic components, but once the stated positional alternatives are available, those details enter the limiting rate rather than removing the logarithmic ceiling. A positive κ∞\kappa_\infty makes the ceiling an asymptotic law, Kreg(N)=κ∞logN+o(logN)K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)=\kappa_\infty\log N+o(\log N).

The coefficient is the infimum of the cost per nat over the digits available, and gives radix economy a physical interpretation. Suppose a monolithic digit of base bb costs cbcb, one element per state [40]. Repeating such digits costs cb/lnbcb/\ln b per nat. Its continuous minimum is at b=eb=e, so the least integer-base price is 3c/ln33c/\ln3, attained by ternary digits. The resulting organisation is logarithmic even though each monolithic register has a linear cost law. No element has been made cheaper, but the same elements have been allowed to count in digits rather than paying for one separate element per distinction. This illustrates why the representational organisation must belong in any account of cost - it can change the growth law of the budget, not merely its prefactor. Of course actual circuit costs can favour binary instead [41], so the relevant comparison is the measured price of each available digit, including the apparatus needed to use it.

Having priced a whole range, we return to the distinction that motivated it: what does it cost to realise an individual symbol? A budget sufficient for every alternative need not be paid in full every time one of them is used. For a fixed organisation write

Kℛ(x)=fℛ(r(x)),K_{\mathcal R}(x)=f_{\mathcal R}\bigl(r(x)\bigr), (27)

and let

X≤r:={x′:r(x′)≤r}.X_{\le r}:=\{x':r(x')\le r\}. (28)

The prefix contains rr distinctions. This connects an individual symbol cost to a register budget: because costs respect the order, a design capable of realising the symbol at rank rr can realise every symbol in its prefix at no greater cost.

The converse construction must include the means of identifying a symbol’s description. A fixed-width positional code reserves the same number of digits for every symbol in its range. A variable-length code can use fewer digits for more typical symbols, but must also record where each description ends. The words of each length form a register, and distinguishing those lengths is itself a register choice. A terminator is one way to make that choice explicit. Its cost belongs in the same account as the digits, so we must check whether paying it changes the leading symbol-cost rate.

Theorem 4 (Cost of a symbol).

Let KregK_{\mathrm{reg}} satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 3 with κ∞>0\kappa_\infty>0, and let an organisation ℛ\mathcal R realise a ranked vocabulary with nondecreasing symbol cost fℛf_{\mathcal R}. (i) If the rr most typical symbols of ℛ\mathcal R form an available organisation for rr distinctions, then

fℛ(r)≥Kreg(r)≥κ∞logrfor every r.f_{\mathcal R}(r)\;\ge\;K_{\mathrm{reg}}(r)\;\ge\;\kappa_\infty\log r \qquad\text{for every }r. (29)

(ii) If a symbol may be realised as a string of digits, paying the register budget of each digit it occupies, then for every integer b≥2b\ge2 there is an organisation with

fℛ(r)≤Kreg(b+1)(logbr+2)for every r.f_{\mathcal R}(r)\;\le\;K_{\mathrm{reg}}(b+1)\,\bigl(\log_b r+2\bigr) \qquad\text{for every }r. (30)

The asymptotic rates limsupr→∞fℛ(r)/logr\limsup_{r\to\infty}f_{\mathcal R}(r)/\log r of such organisations therefore have infimum κ∞\kappa_\infty, and an organisation of rate κ∞+δ\kappa_\infty+\delta satisfies κ∞logr≤fℛ(r)≤(κ∞+δ)logr+o(logr)\kappa_\infty\log r\le f_{\mathcal R}(r)\le(\kappa_\infty+\delta)\log r+o(\log r).

The two parts close the gap between a budget for a range and a cost for one symbol. Part (i) applies to every organisation satisfying the prefix condition. If the symbol at rank rr could be realised for less than the budget needed for rr distinctions, its entire typicality prefix would provide a cheaper admissible register. The lower bound therefore includes the structural cost of reaching the symbol, not just the cost within its final register.

Part (ii) supplies a construction approaching that bound in rate. Use bb letters and one terminator, with each position held in a digit of range b+1b+1. A symbol at rank rr needs at most logbr+2\log_b r+2 positions including its terminator. The resulting rate Kreg(b+1)/logbK_{\mathrm{reg}}(b+1)/\log b approaches κ∞\kappa_\infty as bb grows. Recording where the description ends does not force a larger infimum of leading rates. The complete counting argument is in Appendix C.

This is why the symbol theorem matters. A lower bound alone would leave open whether any code could approach it. An explicit code alone would leave open whether another organisation could do better. The theorem gives both, identifying the same κ∞\kappa_\infty for the efficient register budget and the infimum of achievable symbol-cost rates. Organisation and self-delimitation are included in this comparison rather than supplied as free background machinery.

The terminator is Mandelbrot’s space between words [42]. His logarithmic word cost thus acquires a register-budget basis: the rr most typical words must support rr distinctions, while a positional vocabulary can pay for each word at a rate approaching the register limit. The additional content is that the alphabet and the means of selecting a word are themselves costed registers.

When a finite digit attains the infimum, the length can instead be marked recursively, by writing the number of digits before the digits themselves. Such length coding adds only O(loglogr)O(\log\log r) overhead and can attain the same leading rate [43, 44]. In general the result is an infimum of achievable rates. The leading coefficient is fixed by the register comparison, while the lower-order overhead records how a particular code identifies its words. That distinction will matter at the boundary of an infinite vocabulary.

What additional choice turns the limiting rate into the exact symbol cost used below? Under homogeneous refinement, the ideal prefix-budget identification is

Kℛ(x)=Kreg(r(x)).K_{\mathcal R}(x)=K_{\mathrm{reg}}\bigl(r(x)\bigr). (31)

It assigns no selection overhead beyond the complete budget of the symbol’s typicality prefix. Combined with homogeneous refinement it gives

Kℛ(x)=K0+κlogr(x).K_{\mathcal R}(x)=K_0+\kappa\log r(x). (32)

Self-delimiting codes over a fixed alphabet require lower-order overhead, as the Kraft inequality and universal integer codes make explicit [33, 45]. Thus the exact law supplies an ideal model. Theorem 4 controls the achievable leading rate even when this equality cannot hold at every rank. For κ>0\kappa>0, absorbing the reference cost and unit into later multipliers gives the canonical model

K(r)=logr.K(r)=\log r. (33)

The geometry of refinement

A supremely helpful perspective for understanding this result is the geometric one. First denote Bℋ(N)B_{\mathcal H}(N) for the complete budget of the Hilbert construction detailed below. In this subsection set K0=Bℋ(1)K_0=B_{\mathcal H}(1) and we write g(N)=Bℋ(N)−K0g(N)=B_{\mathcal H}(N)-K_0 for its excess budget. The construction we will employ is that of a Hilbert curve  [46]. We first divide a square into four quadrants and visit them in a fixed order, up the left-hand side, across the top and down the right-hand side. Divide each quadrant into four again and visit its sub-squares in the same pattern, rotated or reflected so that the last sub-square of one quadrant is adjacent to the first sub-square of the next. After kk such steps the path passes once through each of the 4k4^k cells of a 2k×2k2^k\times2^k grid, moving only between neighbouring cells, as in Fig. 3(a). This path is the order-kk Hilbert curve, and as k→∞k\to\infty it converges to a continuous curve through every point of the square. This is the standard example of a space-filling curve, with the useful propert that cells that are close along the path are close in space. This is one reason why such curves are used to order data in spatial databases and large matrix computations [47].

We may number the cells in the order the path visits them, and take this number as the rank of a symbol. The path finishes each quadrant before it enters the next, so the first 4k−14^{k-1} ranks occupy the first quadrant, the next 4k−14^{k-1} the second, and the same holds inside every quadrant at every depth. A rank therefore names a quadrant, then a quadrant within that quadrant, and so on down to a single cell. Every square in the construction is a register for the cells it contains, while being itself one of the four symbols of the register above it. This is the double role of a register made literal. The subdivision of Eq. (6) holds at every depth without reordering a single rank, which is why the sixteen symbols of Fig. 2 could be regrouped along one path. Refinement is just as uniform. Passing from 4k4^k to 4k+14^{k+1} distinctions repeats one motif, a cross dividing a register into four, in each of the 4k4^k registers at depth kk.

The path fixes how the distinctions nest. It leaves open the price of each repetition of the motif, but this is fixed by recursively by the fact that every repetition acts on a smaller square. At depth ii the registers have width wi=2−iw_i=2^{-i} in units of the original square, and there are wi−2=4iw_i^{-2}=4^i of them. Suppose that the price of dividing a register into four depends only on its width, in proportion to wγw^{\gamma}. The square fixes the rate at which its registers narrow, and with it the balance point. Shrink each quadrant by a factor 1/s1/s instead of 1/21/2, keep it in its corner, and repeat. The distinction between these geometric budgets and their efficient envelope is now useful. An architecture can therefore have a superlogarithmic raw cost while the efficient organisation assembled from its registers has a logarithmic asymptote. At this stage, we have used principles of efficiency and recursive self-similarity to obtain the form of representational cost, and made contact with the geometry of space-filling curves. Let us now complete the link to thermodynamics, by specifying what arepresentation physically does.

IV. The Guessing Machine

The preceding section attacked the cost of a representation from its organisation. It compared register budgets, but what does a representation physically do with the distinctions it holds? We now approach the question from this other direction. Consider a machine that proposes an answer, checks it, and starts again when the answer is wrong. Its register must hold a candidate long enough to test it. To reuse that register, the machine must decide what to keep and what to discard. The abstract distinctions of the previous sections have become something the apparatus actually does.

Why choose such an unassuming machine? It exposes the three jobs we want to separate: generating a candidate, verifying it, and clearing the space for another attempt. A deep result in mathematical logic lets us express a broad class of computations in this language. The erasure bound will then attach a physical price to the uncertainty discarded by the chosen protocol.

Computation as Diophantine search

Call a set recursively enumerable if there exists a systematic procedure - an algorithm - that can confirm membership of any element in the set, though it may run indefinitely on non-members [49]. This includes every set for which a positive answer can be confirmed by a computation that terminates. The Davis–Putnam–Robinson–Matiyasevich (DPRM) theorem states that every such set 𝒮⊆ℕm\mathcal S\subseteq\mathbb N^m can be characterised by integer solutions to a polynomial equation:

u∈𝒮⇔∃x∈ℕn:D(u,x)=0,u\in\mathcal S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists x\in\mathbb N^n:D(u,x)=0, (37)

where DD has integer coefficients [50, 51]. Here uu is a tuple of parameters, the input to the computation, and xx is a tuple of witnesses. A witness is a candidate whose existence certifies a positive answer, much as an explicit counterexample certifies a conjecture false. Once a witness is supplied, verification reduces to evaluating D(u,x)D(u,x) and testing whether it vanishes.

The distinction between finding a witness and checking one is the familiar point. The theorem gives a finite verification procedure once the candidate is in hand, not a general bound on the resources required to find it. Its reach is nevertheless striking: every computably confirmable positive answer has such a polynomial witness relation. An integer polynomial can thus stand in for a computation without resembling the program that performs it.

The polynomial encodes the logical relation, and while an implementation may be realised by logic gates, biochemical pathways or neural wiring, it is still described by the DPRM. It does not require the device to use random guesses, and the translation need not preserve its time, memory or heat costs. These are however are most useful properties for making physical bookkeeping explicit. We use them now to construct an example computational protocol.

Guess, test, erase

For fixed input uu, let pup_u be a distribution over the candidate witnesses. The machine repeats the following cycle:

1. Guess. Sample a candidate x∼pux\sim p_u and hold it in the witness register.

2. Test. Evaluate D(u,x)D(u,x) to determine whether the candidate satisfies the constraint.

3. Erase. If the test fails, reset the witness register to a fixed reference state and try again. If it succeeds, retain the answer and halt.

The machine is deliberately unimaginative. Its purpose is to make the physical jobs visible, not to recommend an efficient search algorithm. Independent guesses find a solution almost surely whenever the satisfying witnesses have positive total probability. Exhaustive enumeration is another possible strategy. Improving the guess distribution changes how often the machine succeeds, while changing what it retains changes the information eventually sent to reset.

This also makes the connection to Section II concrete. The physical memory holding the candidate is a register, its logical readouts are the witness symbols xx, and the test specifies which candidates answer the input uu. A witness must be represented in matter before it can be checked. The figure separates that representation from the test and the subsequent reset. Appendix E gives the formal setup and discusses witness encodings.

The cost of a reset

What, then, does the machine pay when it throws away a wrong answer? After a failed test, the register must be made available for the next candidate. The test has told us that the answer was wrong, but it has not generally told us which wrong answer was stored. It is this remaining uncertainty that a reset removes. A register therefore has three distinct attributes: its capacity, the ensemble occupying it, and the protocol used to reset it.

Let p(x)p(x) be the ensemble of logical states entering a complete classical reset. We take a bath initially thermal at T>0T>0 and uncorrelated with the memory, and a protocol that uses no retained side information. With the internal entropy of a logical state restored by the cycle, the memory entropy decreases by kBH(p)k_BH(p). Landauer’s bound is then

⟨Qerase⟩≥kBTH(p),H(p)=−∑xp(x)lnp(x).\langle Q_{\mathrm{erase}}\rangle\ge k_BT H(p), \qquad H(p)=-\sum_xp(x)\ln p(x). (38)

This is the entropy-decrease form of the principle [18, 20]. The point of the reset is that many possible logical states are returned to one reference state. The discarded distinction is which of those states the register held, and its uncertainty is measured by H(p)H(p), rather than by the size of the numeral written in memory.

A witness known to equal a large integer MM has zero logical entropy, however many digits are used to display it. Conversely, a register of capacity NN has entropy lnN\ln N only when its NN states are equally likely. This is why the occupied ensemble must be specified before a register budget is turned into an erasure cost - capacity tells us what the register can distinguish, while the distribution tells us which uncertainty this reset removes.

The typicality ordering introduced in Section II now acquires a physical interpretation. We order the states by how likely they are to enter the reset. Relabelling a witness cannot change this order or the uncertainty removed, even though it can change the integer used to name it. Probability rank therefore supplies the invariant we need. List the positive probabilities in decreasing order, p1≥p2≥⋯p_1\ge p_2\ge\cdots, breaking ties consistently, and define the mean log-rank

ℓ(p):=∑r≥1prlnr.\ell(p):=\sum_{r\ge1}p_r\ln r. (39)

We call this the rank surprisal. The first rr probabilities are each at least prp_r, so

rpr≤∑j=1rpj≤1,−lnpr≥lnr.rp_r\le\sum_{j=1}^r p_j\le1,\qquad -\ln p_r\ge\ln r. (40)

Averaging and applying Eq. (38) gives

⟨Qerase⟩≥kBTH(p)≥kBTℓ(p).\boxed{\langle Q_{\mathrm{erase}}\rangle \ge k_BT H(p)\ge k_BT\ell(p).} (41)

The physical content of the bound is now attached to rank itself. A state at probability rank rr cannot have probability greater than 1/r1/r, so its surprisal cannot be smaller than lnr\ln r. Averaging converts this elementary ordering fact into a lower bound on the uncertainty erased and hence on the mean heat. We do not need the numerical size of a witness or a particular memory architecture to make that comparison. This extends the use of rank from pricing a vocabulary to pricing a specified operation on its occupied states. The distribution must be the one actually erased, under the reset assumptions above. With that ensemble fixed, changing the labels of its states cannot change the bound. For countable ensembles the entropy comparison holds as an inequality of non-negative extended sums. Finite entropy makes all the information quantities finite.

The rank of the known witness MM is one, as it should be. For a uniform ensemble, ℓ=ln(N!)/N=lnN−1+o(1)\ell=\ln(N!)/N=\ln N-1+o(1), so rank surprisal follows the entropy to leading order. Uniformly refining each state into nn sub-states adds lnn\ln n nats independently of the original range. In the reversible isothermal limit its additional reset cost is kBTlnnk_BT\ln n. This provides a physical realisation of homogeneous refinement for a uniform-reset budget. The relevant order in Eq. (41) is always that of the ensemble actually erased. Appendix F develops the uniform-reset model when refinement prices vary with depth.

For the guessing cycle, the relevant ensemble is the one that reaches the reset after failure. Successful witnesses have left the cycle, so they must not be included as though they too were erased at that step.

Let FF denote failure and ηu=Pr(F∣u)\eta_u=\Pr(F\mid u). The ensemble entering reset is puE(x)=Pr(x∣F,u)p^E_u(x)=\Pr(x\mid F,u), rather than the unconditioned guess distribution. The witness-reset contribution per attempt therefore obeys

⟨Qwitness⟩attempt≥ηukBTH(puE)≥ηukBTℓ(puE).\langle Q_{\mathrm{witness}}\rangle_{\mathrm{attempt}} \ge\eta_u k_BT H(p^E_u) \ge\eta_u k_BT\ell(p^E_u). (42)

Testing, control records and resetting a successful output contribute their own costs. If retained classical information ZZ is used during reset, the corresponding bound uses H(X∣Z)H(X\mid Z), and the rank comparison applies within each conditional ensemble. Reversible computation can postpone erasure by keeping this information [52, 20].

A failed guess has not become physically free merely because it was wrong. The machine may keep a record of it - in which case that record occupies another register - or discard it and pay the reset cost of the uncertainty removed. Keeping the record can make future choices better informed, but whether that benefit repays its storage and access costs is a question about the organisation of the whole process. The problem recurs, it is self similar!

The same logical computation can consequently have different physical costs depending on what is retained and when it is reset. The guessing machine gives a concrete operation to which our rank description can be applied. For a fixed erased ensemble, probability rank survives an invertible relabelling of the witnesses, while the detailed apparatus fixes the additional costs. We can now turn the question around: if the mean logarithmic rank cost is specified but the occupation probabilities are not, what distribution should we infer?

V. The Canonical Cost Curve

So far we have asked what it costs to support distinctions and what is paid when uncertainty over them is erased. A register’s use also has statistics: some symbols are encountered often, while others are rare. The cost law does not by itself specify those frequencies. We must distinguish the physical constraint from the inference we make when that constraint is all we know.

We use the canonical symbol cost K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r, selected by homogeneous refinement and the prefix-budget idealisation of Eq. (31). Physical use induces a distribution p(r)p(r) over these states, with mean cost

ℓ=∑rp(r)logr.\ell=\sum_r p(r)\log r. (43)

This is the rank surprisal, the expected logarithmic cost of the occupied symbol. Many distributions share the same mean. Choosing one of them therefore requires an inference rule. If the budget is all we know, how do we avoid supplying additional structure merely because we prefer it?

The rank ensemble

The answer is supplied by Jaynes’ principle of maximum entropy [53]. Among distributions consistent with the stated constraints, it chooses the one that makes the least additional commitment relative to the specified state space and reference measure. This is the sense in which maximum entropy is a requirement of honest inference. The consistency requirements studied by Shore and Johnson provide a related axiomatic basis [54].

Here the reference is counting measure on the ranks: before conditioning on cost, each available rank receives the same weight. A different density of available states would instead enter as a prior weight. With this choice made, we maximise the Shannon entropy of a vocabulary of size RR,

S[p]=−∑r=1Rp(r)logp(r)S[p]=-\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log p(r) (44)

under

∑r=1Rp(r)=1,∑r=1Rp(r)logr=ℓ.\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)=1,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log r=\ell. (45)

The multiplier λ\lambda conjugate to mean cost prices departures towards more expensive ranks. The maximum-entropy weight of a state is exponential in its cost. Since that cost is logarithmic, e−λlogr=r−λe^{-\lambda\log r}=r^{-\lambda}: the exponential in cost becomes a power law in rank. Normalisation gives

p(r∣λ)=r−λZR(λ),p(r\mid\lambda)=\frac{r^{-\lambda}}{Z_R(\lambda)}, (46)

where

ZR(λ)=∑r=1Rr−λ.Z_R(\lambda)=\sum_{r=1}^R r^{-\lambda}. (47)

Theorem 5 (Zipf equilibrium).

Let R≥2R\ge2 and K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r on {1,…,R}\{1,\dots,R\}. For 0<ℓ<logR0<\ell<\log R, the maximum-entropy distribution at fixed mean cost ℓ\ell is p(r∣λ)=r−λ/ZR(λ)p(r\mid\lambda)=r^{-\lambda}/Z_R(\lambda). The equation of state ℓ(λ)=−∂λlogZR(λ)\ell(\lambda)=-\partial_\lambda\log Z_R(\lambda) uniquely determines finite λ\lambda. The endpoint ensembles are obtained as λ→±∞\lambda\to\pm\infty.

The theorem says that a power law need not be supplied as a further guess about the occupation statistics. It is the canonical maximum-entropy weight of a logarithmically priced rank. The calculation, together with its extension to scale-covariant costs, is given in Appendix G. For rank to agree with decreasing occupation probability we use λ≥0\lambda\ge0, with λ=0\lambda=0 giving uniform occupation.

Mandelbrot obtained related power laws by assigning logarithmic word costs [42]. The preceding register and symbol theorems explain where the leading rate of that cost can come from once the organisation is itself included. The canonical model goes further by taking the logarithmic symbol law to be exact. Under that cost and the stated mean constraint, the occupation law is then fixed rather than fitted independently. This is the sequence of the argument: composition constrains the cost, and maximum entropy turns that cost into a distribution. Moreover, this rank law is not tied to a single mechanism. Power law distributions have been derived in a variety of previous contexts  [55, 56]. Our contribution is to establish the minimal systemic properties under which these Zipfian distributions obtain. The fact that these describe so many biological and societal phenomena is extremely suggestive. Consider for example that word frequencies in many languages exhibit a power law distribution with exponents close to λ=1\lambda=1. From this we may infer two facts. First, that natural language is close to an optimal representation under additive composition [Footnote 3. one could argue that language creation is a form of subadditive composition. No cap.] . Secondly, its exponent tells us that we humans and bots are prepared to pay an enormous cost for linguistic facility. Understanding why this last statement is true must be true is our next concern.

The cost curve

What does this distribution allow us to calculate? The partition sum is more than a normalising constant. Changing λ\lambda shifts weight between cheap and expensive ranks, and derivatives of its logarithm tell us how the mean cost responds. Substituting the same probabilities into the entropy gives a second quantity along that curve:

C(λ):=−ddλlogZR(λ)=ℓ(λ),C(\lambda):=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log Z_R(\lambda)=\ell(\lambda), (48)

Free complexity packages this competition between cost and the breadth of occupation in the familiar canonical form. The statistical mechanics is already recognisable, although a physical bath temperature enters only when we specify an operation such as the reset of Section IV.

The boundary λ=1\lambda=1 marks the point at which suppression of expensive states no longer compensates for the unbounded range of states available. No normalised infinite ensemble exists there.

VI. Outlook

Every representation has to be built out of something, but this does not fix how its constituents must be organised. Representation can change without changing the target it represents. The sixteen symbols of Fig. 2 make this freedom explicit: a smaller choice within a register must be paid for by a larger choice of register. Once both are included, the problem repeats at the level of the apparatus itself. This is the central purpose of the recursive account developed here, to give the organisation of a representation a cost that can be compared with the cost of its contents.

A useful way to view the logarithmic result is through the freedom to change coordinates. A monolithic register may be expensive to enlarge, yet its components can support the same distinctions more economically when used as digits. The asymptotic price is set by the infimum of the costs per nat available to that construction. The register theorem supplies both a ceiling from every available digit and a common limiting rate. The symbol theorem then shows that paying for selection and termination need not increase the infimum of leading rates. The generalisation is therefore not just a logarithm assigned to another kind of symbol. It is a comparison of physical organisations that prices the machinery needed to make those symbols accessible. The geometric examples show what changes before this optimisation: the number of subdivisions grows while the resource consumed by each subdivision shrinks. Their balance fixes the refinement law. The logarithm is the case in which every multiplicative increase of range has the same price. On this account, the logarithm is the cost of counting with physical registers.

That logarithmic coordinate also gives the Zipf boundary a concrete meaning. In the finite harmonic distribution, each octave of rank carries approximately the same probability. The vocabulary uses many scales without selecting one of them as special. A direct physical example is the harmonic entanglement catalyst [58], whose ordered Schmidt probabilities are

pr=1rHR,HR=∑j=1R1j.p_r=\frac{1}{rH_R},\qquad H_R=\sum_{j=1}^R\frac1j. (61)

Its organisation across scales enables catalytic transformations. The finite cutoff is part of that construction, just as it is part of a physical register. In the classical reset model, enlarging this harmonic vocabulary raises the entropy removed per complete reset as 12lnR+lnlnR+O(1)\frac12\ln R+\ln\ln R+O(1).

The question of useful complexity is equally concrete in physical learning. Learning capacity has been repeatedly linked to dynamic phases [59, 60], while the response of an accessible subsystem is linked to spectral nonflatness and metrological sensitivity [61]. In these systems, generating more structure is useful only insofar as the available readout can use it. This gives the target-relative cost account a practical setting: compare the information preserved about the same task, the organisation needed to access it, and the resources consumed by preparing, reading and resetting that organisation. Furthermore, it emphasises the view of computation as a form of dynamics.

Where does this leave learning? Representation learning already makes such choices by finding variables in which useful structure becomes accessible [62]. Architecture search [63] and pruning [4, 5] extend the choice to the apparatus holding those variables. The present theory supplies a static comparison between the resulting organisations. A dynamical theory must then price the changes between them, including the information retained along the way. This is where the nearly decomposable hierarchy  [64] becomes a physical design question: when does the saving within a component repay the cost of selecting and coordinating it?

Recursive self-improvement makes that question unavoidable. The procedure that proposes a change can itself be rewritten [6, 7, 8]. Its evaluator, archive and memory belong to the same physical account. Keeping unsuccessful candidates can make later searches more informative, but requires storage and access. Discarding them releases capacity while erasing uncertainty about the history. The trade-off concerns the entire process by which improvement is achieved. Connecting these static budgets to learning rates will require the duration and dissipation of the allowed operations [32, 23]. With those ingredients, whether such costs bound the rate of self-improvement becomes a well-posed physical question.

The cost of complexity is therefore also a question of where complexity is placed. The same distinctions can be prohibitively expensive in one organisation and accessible in another. Any universal theory of learning must account for that freedom, including when the learner turns it on itself. When it comes to accounting, the universe makes no special exceptions. Finally, more recursively minded readers may have noticed that this theory of representation applies even to itself, and to the best of our knowledge remains consistent with itself. Just imagine what it cost to organise.

Appendix J — The cost–entropy–fidelity functional

With WW the source variable and fixed p(ω,y)p(\omega,y), each encoder q(x∣ω)q(x\mid\omega) induces a different marginal pq(x)p_q(x) and target information. Dropping constraint constants from the negative of the maximum-entropy Lagrangian gives

𝒥[q]=λC[q]−Hq(X)−βIq(X;Y).\mathcal J[q]=\lambda C[q]-H_q(X)-\beta I_q(X;Y). (J1)

This is the stated Lagrange functional. A nonconvex encoder class need not allow every constrained optimum to be recovered by a choice of multipliers. For a fixed K(x)K(x) and finite Z(λ)Z(\lambda), expansion of relative entropy gives

D(pq‖ρλ)=−Hq(X)+λC[q]+lnZ(λ),D(p_q\Vert\rho_\lambda) =-H_q(X)+\lambda C[q]+\ln Z(\lambda), (J2)

which proves Eq. (58). This identity needs no assumption that pqp_q is an equilibrium.

For deterministic encoders and the additional calibration C[q]=aHq(X)+bC[q]=aH_q(X)+b, one obtains

𝒥[q]=(λa−1)Iq(W;X)−βIq(X;Y)+λb.\mathcal J[q] =(\lambda a-1)I_q(W;X)-\beta I_q(X;Y)+\lambda b. (J3)

A positive rescaling yields Eq. (60) if λa>1\lambda a>1. A lower bound on C[q]C[q] in terms of entropy is insufficient to establish equality of the two optimisation problems. An asymptotic coding interpretation requires the calibration error to be controlled across the candidate class, as well as the blocklength limit.

For stochastic encoders, Hq(X)=Iq(W;X)+Hq(X∣W)H_q(X)=I_q(W;X)+H_q(X\mid W). Retaining the entropy calibration therefore adds (λa−1)Hq(X∣W)(\lambda a-1)H_q(X\mid W) to the deterministic expression. Alternatively, calibrating cost to source information, C[q]=aIq(W;X)+bC[q]=aI_q(W;X)+b, gives

𝒥[q]=(λa−1)Iq(W;X)−Hq(X∣W)−βIq(X;Y)+λb.\begin{aligned} \mathcal J[q]={}&(\lambda a-1)I_q(W;X) -H_q(X\mid W)\\ &-\beta I_q(X;Y)+\lambda b. \end{aligned} (J4)

In either case the conditional entropy must be fixed, vanish, or be explicitly offset by another cost term to recover the same standard bottleneck over stochastic encoders. That is a distinct modelling choice, not an algebraic consequence of rank-cost maximum entropy.
