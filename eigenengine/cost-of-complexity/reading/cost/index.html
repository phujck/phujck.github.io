Abstract

Every representation must be embodied in matter, and this embodiment carries a cost. The cost extends beyond the symbols being manipulated to the structure of the registers that hold them, so the same distinctions can be prohibitively expensive in one organisation and accessible in another. We ask what constrains this freedom when the apparatus that organises a representation must itself be represented, and make three contributions. First, we show that efficiency and recursive self-consistency alone fix the form of representational cost: under additive positional composition the efficient register budget grows logarithmically, symbol costs can approach the same rate, and maximum entropy at fixed mean cost yields the Zipf family of distributions. Second, we establish a correspondence between this cost and the geometry of space-filling curves, in which the exponent of a refinement law is set by the dimension of the resource each division consumes and can be read from how often a small perturbation changes a register’s address. Third, we realise the cost thermodynamically in a Diophantine guessing machine, a minimal model of general computation whose heat of erasure is bounded below by the mean logarithm of probability rank. Together these results trace the logarithmic structure common to information theory, thermodynamics and Zipf’s law to a single origin in the efficient embodiment of distinctions. They offer a foundation for a covering theory across these fields, in which the organisation of a representation is itself a physical object of optimisation.

I. Introduction

The most successful theories in science are organised around a balancing act. In classical mechanics, kinetic and potential terms enter the same action. In statistical mechanics, equilibrium reflects a balance between energy and entropy, while learning theory examines how model capacity shapes bias and variance [1, 2] and the trade-off between compression and fidelity [3]. The quantities being balanced belong to different ontologies, yet the recurrence of competing demands invites a common question: what does a representation have to carry, and what does it cost to carry it?

Any answer must account for the physical embodiment of information, as there exists no Platonic register upon which dynamics can act. Theories are used through substrates that store, manipulate and erase distinctions under finite resource budgets. A substrate-independent account must therefore describe those distinctions generally enough to connect different fields, while leaving each description its own interpretation and notion of efficiency.

This question has become harder to ignore in the present moment. Large-scale machine learning makes the resource constraints plain, motivating the search for more efficient representations of models [4, 5]. Practical success does not diminish the need for a theory of the process. Steam engines could be realised before thermodynamics, but their operation gave the theory something concrete to explain. History is now repeating itself in the age of information engines, and the approaching era of recursive self-improvement makes the need for a theoretical description particularly urgent. In both proof-guided and empirically evaluated approaches, the procedure performing the optimisation can itself be modified [6, 7, 8]. The physical account must therefore include the machinery that proposes, evaluates and retains revisions, as well as the representation being revised.

We address the static part of that account. Our starting point is a representation equipped with an order of typicality and a physical budget for realising its symbols. The additional observation is that a register has two roles: it holds distinctions at one level and is itself selected by a distinction at the next. This recursion brings the organisation of a representation into the same cost account as its contents. When registers can be composed positionally at additive cost, their efficient budget has a well-defined asymptotic price per nat. A positive price gives logarithmic growth, while homogeneous refinement makes the law exact.

Representation has long played this organising role in physics. Renormalisation preserves large-scale descriptions while discarding microscopic detail [9], and predictive-state constructions retain the distinctions needed for future prediction [10]. Influence functionals recast environmental effects as stochastic open-system dynamics [11, 12] and provide a route to mean-force Hamiltonians [13]. Other transformations relate imaginary-time evolution to ensembles of unitary dynamics [14, 15], map free to harmonic motion in quantum and Koopman dynamics [16], or expose quantum–classical correspondence through the waveoperator [17]. Each makes some structure accessible by changing how it is represented. The cost of choosing and supporting that representation is the concern of the present work.

Several literatures already orbit aspects of this problem. Landauer’s principle relates the entropy removed by reset to its heat cost [18, 19, 20], while efficient coding asks how signals can be organised economically into useful variables [21]. The information bottleneck makes utility explicit through a target whose information must be preserved [22]. Closer to physics, the thermodynamics of prediction connects predictive organisation to dissipation [23], and resource theories specify which transformations the available operations can achieve [24, 25]. Finally constructor theory formulates physical constraints in terms of possible and impossible transformations [26, 27]. Together these approaches make clear why a theory of representation needs to account for both the cost of distinctions represented, and the operations through which they become useful.

Section II introduces the representation, its target and the efficiency assumptions. In Section III we develop the recursive cost account, derive the register and symbol bounds, and show that the same account describes the geometry of space-filling curves, in which the exponent of a refinement law is set by the dimension of the resource each division consumes. Section IV connects probability rank to the heat of erasure, using Diophantine search to relate a guessing machine to general computation. Section V derives the conditional Zipf family from maximum entropy at fixed mean cost. We conclude in Section VI with representations whose target includes themselves, and with the consequences for physical learning and recursive self-improvement. The individual proofs, technical extensions and a Monte Carlo test of the perimeter readout are collected in Appendices A–J.

II. Representations in Matter

Our starting point is deliberately sparse. A representation must be realised in some physical system, and its use draws on the resources of that system. This applies to the apparatus that organises the representation as much as to the symbols it holds [Footnote 1. There are no exceptions. It applies just as much to the theory proposed here as to any domain of application. This makes everything that follows unavoidably self-referential, and more than a bit meta.] . Thermodynamic accounts of computation make these resources explicit through the operations performed, their duration and the states available to implement them [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. We therefore seek a description of structural organisation before choosing a particular substrate.

To explore the consequences of embodiment, we must first establish what is being represented and what carries the representation. Let Ω\Omega be the space of microscopic configurations relevant to the problem, and let y:Ω→Yy:\Omega\to Y specify the target distinctions. The target need not be a complete microscopic description. It might ask which of sixteen intervals contains an instrument reading, without asking for the positions of every atom in the instrument.

We call a physical system a register when its distinguishable states are used to realise a set of alternatives. The term is not restricted to computer memory. The position of a pointer, the level of an electrical signal, or a pattern across several physical elements can all serve this purpose. A register may therefore be a single device or an organised collection of devices. Its range is the number of alternatives it can distinguish at the resolution required by the task.

We write RR for the physical register and XX for the set of symbols it contains. These play different roles - RR is the apparatus, while x∈Xx\in X names one of the distinctions realised by that apparatus. Sixteen symbols need not require sixteen separate objects physically, they simply represent sixteen distinct objects in the theory being embodied in the register. The readout from the register specifies which physical configurations count as the same symbol. We express this through the symbol map

π:Ω→X.\pi:\Omega\longrightarrow X. (1)

The configurations in Ax=π−1(x)A_x=\pi^{-1}(x) all realise the same symbol. Microscopic changes within AxA_x leave the readout unchanged, so the symbol map treats those configurations as indistinguishable. This is the coarse-graining supplied by the physical embodiment of the theory being represented. The representation is faithful to the target when π(ω)=π(ω′)\pi(\omega)=\pi(\omega') implies y(ω)=y(ω′)y(\omega)=y(\omega'). It may then discard microscopic detail while retaining everything required to describe the target. For a probabilistic target the corresponding condition is that its conditional distribution depend on ω\omega only through π(ω)\pi(\omega), the familiar sufficiency condition [33]. When the target is the distribution of future observations, this leads to the causal-state construction of computational mechanics [10].

Critically, the target may include the register itself. This is the situation faced by a system that represents its own organisation, and it requires the apparatus to remain inside the resource account. Exact fidelity is still a question about the chosen target: a one-bit register can preserve a binary property of a much larger system. In the case that the target theory being represented demands more distinctions than the available register can hold, the representation must choose what to retain. There are naturally limits on the capacity of a physical device to faithfully represent its target, which are considered at length within the inference-device framework. This treats observation, prediction and recollection within a common formal account [34], and supplies limits to the fidelity with which a target system can be physically modeled. We therefore take the target as given and instead ask what it costs to support the distinctions needed to represent it.

To reach x11x_{11} in the arrangement shown, we first select the third subregister, then its third symbol. The choice of register is itself a distinction that must be physically represented however, as changing the organisation leaves the meaning of x11x_{11} intact, but changes how it is reached, and therefore the cost incurred. The price of embodiment

A symbol map tells us which distinction is represented, but not what must be provided to realise it. We therefore assign a realisation cost Kℛ(x)K_{\mathcal R}(x) to symbol xx in an organisation ℛ\mathcal R. Here ℛ\mathcal R specifies the arrangement of registers, the assignment of symbols to their states, and the means of selecting among them. A particular register RR is one component of that organisation. Changing ℛ\mathcal R can change the cost of reaching xx without changing what xx represents. The particulars of where this cost stems from are irrelevant to the argument, we require only that it exists. Physically this requirement is easily motivated by the Landauer bound on information erasure [18, 19, 20], where erasing an unbiased bit at temperature TT has a mean heat floor kBTln2k_BT\ln2. This bound has been confirmed experimentally at length [35, 36, 37], and in Sec. IV we will employ it to construct an operational realisation of the results obtained. For the time being however we will proceed with maximum generality, and use only the fact that physical manipulations are never free.

What makes one allocation of these costs more efficient than another? A representation is more than a set of labels - its symbols stand in correspondence with the distinctions encountered in the world that the theory attempts to describe. The mapping between symbols and events is arbitrary, so the minimum constraining structure on the representation must concern not just the symbols we ascribe, but the frequency with which they are employed. Rather than assume a complete probability measure, we shall rely only on the weaker notion of typicality,

x≽x′⇔x is at least as typical as x′.x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{$x$ is at least as typical as $x'$}. (2)

Typicality records which symbols are more prevalent or readily encountered. It supplies a sense in which symbols can be ordered, without requiring the additional constraint of a measure. We now consider a finite vocabulary or a countable sequence in which every symbol has finitely many predecessors. For the sake of simplicity we shall assume distinct typicalities in symbols, to avoid prescribing any otherwise arbitrary procedure for breaking ties. This typicality order can be represented by a scalar τ\tau, but changing its numerical scale leaves the ordering intact. Our argument must therefore depend on that order rather than on an arbitrary numerical encoding of it.

With typicality established, the consequences of demanding an efficient representation can be formalised. Specifically, efficiency requires that the cost Kℛ(x)K_{\mathcal R}(x) of realising a symbol xx respect the typicality order of the symbols themselves:

x≽x′⇒Kℛ(x)≤Kℛ(x′).x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\mathcal R}(x)\le K_{\mathcal R}(x'). (3)

A representation need not be efficient of course, but there is little interesting to be learned from the study of the badly implemented. Absent other constraints, more frequently encountered events should correspond to more cheaply realisable symbols. Eq. (3) expresses this condition, retaining only the ordinal part of widely used assignment arguments [38, 39, 33]. Efficiency therefore places a constraint on the cost of realising a symbol, but what about the cost of the register itself? This too must be accounted for, and we must include the cost of selecting a register alongside the cost of distinguishing its contents.

III. The Cost of Complexity

Typicality orders the distinctions a representation carries. We now ask how their costs depend on the registers through which they are reached. The typicality prefix of xx is the vocabulary taken in typicality order down to xx, including any symbols tied with it. When every such prefix is finite, define the rank of a symbol by

r(x):=#{x′∈X:x′≽x},r(x):=\#\{x'\in X:x'\succcurlyeq x\}, (4)

such that with distinct typicalities this is the ordinal position of xx. An order-preserving change of the typicality scale leaves it unchanged.

Observation (Rank gauge).

For an order-respecting representation on such a vocabulary, there is a nondecreasing function fℛf_{\mathcal R} such that

Kℛ(x)=fℛ(r(x)).K_{\mathcal R}(x)=f_{\mathcal R}\bigl(r(x)\bigr). (5)

Each rank identifies one symbol, so this is the cost function expressed in the invariant coordinate supplied by the order. It does not yet fix the function fℛf_{\mathcal R}. We must now ask how an efficient representation behaves under composition. It is tempting to assume additivity immediately, but that would miss the crucial insight upon which the rest of this work turns. That is, to account for the cost of providing and selecting the registers themselves.

There are two ways to accommodate more distinctions. We can enlarge the range of an existing register, or distribute the distinctions across additional registers. The latter makes the choice within each register smaller, but introduces another choice: which register contains the required symbol? If that choice is omitted from the account, every symbol could be given a register of its own and the internal cost would appear to disappear. At the other extreme, a single universal register makes selection trivial but must support all the distinctions internally. A meaningful comparison must account for both tasks.

The original problem therefore reappears at the level of the apparatus used to organise the symbols. To make this self-similarity explicit, take the first N=mnN=mn symbols in rank order and divide them into mm consecutive subregisters of nn symbols. The original rank can now be identified by the subregister index ii and the position jj within it:

r=n(i−1)+j,1≤i≤m,1≤j≤n.r=n(i-1)+j,\qquad 1\le i\le m,\quad1\le j\le n. (6)

This is a positional encoding : one choice among mnmn alternatives becomes a choice among mm registers followed by a choice among nn symbols. The two indices identify exactly the same distinction as the original rank, without requiring statistical independence between them. We illustrate this in Fig. 2 by returning to the sixteen symbols of Fig. 1. There, two registers of eight symbols and eight registers of two symbols both carry the same sixteen distinctions. The first index now exposes the recursion. At the level where registers are selected, ii is itself a symbol. Once that selection has been made, the chosen register supplies the space within which jj is distinguished. Depending on the altitude at which one considers it, a register is therefore both the container for a distinction, and a distinguishable alternative in its own right. Moving the boundary between these roles changes the organisation without changing which symbol is ultimately identified. Our problem is therefore self-similar, a fact which we can exploit.

Let us first make the two contributions to the cost explicit. Fix a physical representation ℛ\mathcal R, let RxR_x denote the register containing symbol xx. The complete cost includes both providing the apparatus through which RxR_x is selected and distinguishing xx once that register has been selected:

Kℛ(x)=Cℛ(Rx)+Kℛ(x∣Rx).K_{\mathcal R}(x) =C_{\mathcal R}(R_x)+K_{\mathcal R}(x\mid R_x). (7)

We call Cℛ(Rx)C_{\mathcal R}(R_x) the structural cost, accounting for all the costs of realisation outside of the cost of the symbol itself. The conditional contribution Kℛ(x∣Rx)K_{\mathcal R}(x\mid R_x) is then the remaining cost of using the symbol xx within that structure, with the vertical bar expressing its conditional dependence on the selected register.

Note that the form of Eq. (7) is not arbitrary, but is forced upon us as soon as we demand recursive self-consistency. Explicitly, if the register RxR_x can itself be treated as a distinction in a higher-level register SS, the decomposition of costs must respect this. This means that the structural cost of RxR_x must obey

Cℛ(Rx)=Cℛ(S)+Kℛ(Rx∣S),C_{\mathcal R}(R_x)=C_{\mathcal R}(S)+K_{\mathcal R}(R_x\mid S), (8)

identically to the form of Eq. (7). Together they force a chain rule for the complete cost:

Kℛ(x)=Cℛ(S)+Kℛ(Rx∣S)+Kℛ(x∣Rx).\begin{aligned} K_{\mathcal R}(x) &=C_{\mathcal R}(S)+K_{\mathcal R}(R_x\mid S)\\ &\quad+K_{\mathcal R}(x\mid R_x). \end{aligned} (9)

Again we emphasise that Eq. (7) is simply the unique recursively self-consistent accounting identity for cost. It does not rely on any assumption on the form of composition, nor a probabilistic model. Its form however is - not coincidentally - familiar to any practitioner of thermodynamics or information theory. Here we see that it arises in any consistent treatment of constrained resources, and the particular equation of state it realises depends on the specific demands the theory imposes.

What distinguishes the present case is that it concerns the demands efficiency imposes on representation itself. This makes the question of how representations compose together critical, as it determines how the two terms of cost change in relation to each other. Moving symbols into a smaller register may make them cheaper to distinguish internally, but it also changes the selection through which they are reached. A saving in the first task is a saving overall only if it pays for the change in the second. The limiting cases make this particularly clear. One universal register makes the selector trivial and leaves every distinction inside it. Giving each symbol its own register instead produces the one-to-one correspondence

x↔Rx.x\longleftrightarrow R_x. (10)

There is then no choice left within a selected register, but selecting that register requires precisely the original distinctions, with their original typicality order. The representational problem has moved into CℛC_{\mathcal R}, forcing our initial question to recur one level higher.

Composing Costs

We can now see how our question begins to stack. A proper accounting of efficiency requires both symbol cost and register cost, but the form these two costs take is necessarily distinct. In the former case it must respect the typicality of a symbol, but the cost of a register is linked to the budget for the entire vocabulary it holds. To support a range of NN alternatives, a register must be able to realise any one of them, including the most expensive. For a prescribed organisation ℛ\mathcal R, this requires the largest complete symbol cost on that range.

Deciding whether a representation is efficient necessitates comparison between different organisations of the same distinctions. Let 𝒜N\mathcal A_N be the admissible organisations supporting the first NN symbols in the common typicality order, with all structural costs included. The choice of this class specifies which physical designs and compositions are available. We distinguish the budget of a particular design from the infimum over those alternatives:

Bℛ(N):=maxr(x)≤NKℛ(x),Kreg(N):=infℛ∈𝒜NBℛ(N).\begin{aligned} B_{\mathcal R}(N)&:=\max_{r(x)\le N}K_{\mathcal R}(x),\\ K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)&:=\inf_{\mathcal R\in\mathcal A_N}B_{\mathcal R}(N). \end{aligned} (11)

The maximum in BℛB_{\mathcal R} acts on the complete cost for the same symbol, including both terms of Eq. (7). It is a budget sufficient for any alternative in the vocabulary, not the sum of the costs of realising all alternatives simultaneously. The second quantity, KregK_{\mathrm{reg}}, is the efficient envelope of the available designs. Its infimum need not be attained by a single representation [Footnote 2. In future work we shall use this to establish the theory’s connections to non-commutative theories such as quantum mechanics.] . We assume finite budgets and allow an organisation supporting MM symbols to serve any smaller prefix, retaining the full cost of its unused capacity. A sixteen-state register can therefore be used for its first ten alternatives without pretending that the other six states cost nothing to provide. This restriction property then lets us compare vocabularies of different sizes, and yields the following theorem:

Theorem 1 (Monotonicity of the register budget).

Under this restriction property,

N≤M⇒Kreg(N)≤Kreg(M).N\le M\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)\le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(M). (12)

Any admissible organisation for MM is an available alternative for N≤MN\le M, at no larger maximum cost on that prefix. Taking the infimum over the alternatives proves the inequality. It is important to emphasise that this inequality bounds the complete budget given by Eq. (7), including both the register structural cost, and the cost of symbols within them.

With this, we are now in a position to formulate the cost of a register based on the range of distinctions it may hold. To do so, we consider a register with a vocabulary of size mm, and refine each of its mm distinctions into nn alternatives, such that the total range is mnmn. That is, distinctions compose multiplicatively. Now let K0=Kreg(1)K_0=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1) be the reference budget for a register carrying a single distinction. We call this the existence cost K0K_0, as even when no internal choice remains, the apparatus can carry a cost. The additional budget for the refinement is the difference

K0:=Kreg(1),Δm(n):=Kreg(mn)−Kreg(m).K_0:=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1),\qquad \Delta_m(n):=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn)-K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m). (13)

Deferring the question of path dependence to a full dynamic treatment, we stipulate that the budget difference depends only on the end points. Successive differences then telescope,

Δm(np)=Δm(n)+Δmn(p),\Delta_m(np)=\Delta_m(n)+\Delta_{mn}(p), (14)

without requiring a model of the work needed to rebuild the register. The price of a refinement may still depend on the range already supported, and we express this by defining ϵ\epsilon as the differential cost less the existence cost:

ϵ(m,n):=Δm(n)−Δ1(n),\epsilon(m,n):=\Delta_m(n)-\Delta_1(n), (15)

which gives the general composition identity

Kreg(mn)=Kreg(m)+Kreg(n)−K0+ϵ(m,n).\begin{aligned} K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn) &=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m)+K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)\\ &\quad-K_0+\epsilon(m,n). \end{aligned} (16)

The definition makes ϵ\epsilon symmetric, and consistent under successive composition:

ϵ(m,n)=ϵ(n,m),\epsilon(m,n)=\epsilon(n,m), (17)
ϵ(m,n)+ϵ(mn,p)=ϵ(n,p)+ϵ(m,np).\begin{aligned} &\epsilon(m,n)+\epsilon(mn,p)\\ &\qquad=\epsilon(n,p)+\epsilon(m,np). \end{aligned} (18)

ϵ(m,n)\epsilon(m,n) measures the possible corrections to additivity any self-consistent representation may maintain. Unsurprisingly, its properties are identical to those of mutual information, I(A;B)=H(A)+H(B)−H(A,B)I(A;B)=H(A)+H(B)-H(A,B) [33].

Pricing Refinement

The simplest case occurs when the cost of a refinement has the same relative price irrespective of the range of distinctions the register already supports, i.e. when distinctions multiply but costs add. In the notation just introduced,

Δm(n)=Δ1(n)for all m,n∈ℕ.\Delta_m(n)=\Delta_1(n)\qquad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb N. (19)

We call this homogeneous refinement. Each multiplication of the supported range has the same price, whatever the range already present. With

g(n):=Kreg(n)−K0,g(n):=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)-K_0, (20)

the composition identity becomes

g(mn)=g(m)+g(n).g(mn)=g(m)+g(n). (21)

Theorem 2 (Logarithmic register cost).

Let Kreg:ℕ→ℝ≥0K_{\mathrm{reg}}:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R_{\ge0} be finite and nondecreasing, and let its conditional increments satisfy Eq. (19). Then

Kreg(n)=K0+κlogn,K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n)=K_0+\kappa\log n, (22)

where

κ=Kreg(2)−Kreg(1)log2≥0.\kappa = \frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(2)-K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1)} {\log 2} \ge0. (23)

Moreover, κ>0\kappa>0 if and only if Kreg(2)>Kreg(1)K_{\mathrm{reg}}(2)>K_{\mathrm{reg}}(1).

This result fixes the entire cost law, not only its large-range limit. Every doubling adds the same budget κlog2\kappa\log2, and every multiplication by nn adds κlogn\kappa\log n, whatever the range already supported. Monotonicity and homogeneous refinement suffice to determine this, even without a probability measure or an assumed code length. The proof in Appendix A brackets nkn^k between powers of two to fix g(n)/logng(n)/\log n.

Homogeneous refinement is not required for recursive consistency, and the particulars of embodiment may impose scale-dependent costs on the vocabulary available to a representation. We have already seen however that the form of this cost will depend only on Δ\Delta. To see this, take a representation with register budget B(N)B(N) whose differential cost is ΔmB(n)=B(mn)−B(m)\Delta^B_m(n)=B(mn)-B(m). Suppose the price of the same refinement changes with scale according to

ΔqmB(n)=qαΔmB(n)\Delta^B_{qm}(n)=q^\alpha\Delta^B_m(n) (24)

For a nonzero refinement price, consistency under composition then gives the scale-covariant family

B(N)=B(1)+{καNα−1α,α≠0,κ0logN,α=0.B(N)=B(1)+ \begin{cases} \displaystyle\kappa_\alpha\frac{N^\alpha-1}{\alpha}, &\alpha\ne0,\\[1ex] \kappa_0\log N,&\alpha=0. \end{cases} (25)

where homogeneous refinement corresponds to α=0\alpha=0. Appendix D derives this family and shows how the power-law scaling factor follows from a monotone dependence on scale ratios. The excess budget is καlnqN\kappa_\alpha\ln_q N, where q=1−αq=1-\alpha and lnq\ln_q is the qq-logarithm . While formally α\alpha can take any real value, negative α\alpha implies an unbounded set of distinctions for a bounded price, and should be regarded as unphysical. With this established, we are in a position to derive more general bounds on register costs.

Bounding Register Costs

So far we have resisted assuming additive composition of register costs, but ultimately all theories must draw a boundary of independence between their elements at some scale. If two registers with ranges mm and nn are physically independent, they will support mnmn distinctions at the sum of their complete budgets. If arbitrarily near-optimal registers at mm and nn can be combined in this way, the efficient budget for mnmn distinctions cannot exceed the sum of the separate budgets:

Kreg(mn)≤Kreg(m)+Kreg(n),K_{\mathrm{reg}}(mn)\le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(m)+K_{\mathrm{reg}}(n), (26)

The range of distinctions per register can now stay fixed while the total range grows. Iterating Eq. (26), kk registers of range bb can support bkb^k distinctions with a budget no greater than kKreg(b)kK_{\mathrm{reg}}(b). To support NN distinctions it is therefore enough to provide ⌈logbN⌉\lceil\log_b N\rceil such registers. From this, we may place bounds on the cost of an efficient representation’s register at any scale:

Theorem 3 (Asymptotic price of a register).

Let KregK_{\mathrm{reg}} be nondecreasing and satisfy Eq. (26) for all m,n∈ℕm,n\in\mathbb N. Then the limit

κ∞:=limN→∞Kreg(N)logN=infN≥2Kreg(N)logN\kappa_\infty := \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)}{\log N} = \inf_{N\ge2}\frac{K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)}{\log N} (27)

exists, and for every N≥2N\ge2 and every integer b≥2b\ge2,

κ∞logN≤Kreg(N)≤Kreg(b)⌈logbN⌉.\kappa_\infty\log N \le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N) \le K_{\mathrm{reg}}(b)\,\lceil\log_b N\rceil. (28)

Appendix B gives the proof. Under homogeneous refinement the lower bound becomes an equality, with κ≡κ∞\kappa\equiv \kappa_\infty (Theorem 2). Moreover, excluding the possibility of a free lunch requires κ∞>0\kappa_\infty>0, meaning the register cost has the leading form Kreg(N)=κ∞logN+o(logN)K_{\mathrm{reg}}(N)=\kappa_\infty\log N+o(\log N). The upper bound is also informative, as it neatly captures the concept of radix economy [40]. For a register whose budget grows in proportion to its range, the bound is least at b=eb=e among real ranges, and so at b=3b=3 among integer ones. No individual element has become cheaper, but a saving is obtained by allowing combinations of registers to carry the distinctions, rather than providing a separate element for each distinction in the combined range. The Canonical Cost

Having constrained the price of the representation that contains the whole range of distinctions, we are finally able to return to the question of an individual symbol’s cost within that representation. The rr most typical symbols, which make up the typicality prefix of the symbol at rank rr, are themselves an organisation of rr distinctions, and none of them costs more than that symbol. Translating this into a rank cost once again exploits the self-similarity of the problem. A variable-length description can use fewer digits for more typical symbols, but must also record where each description ends. Symbols of equal length then form a register, and distinguishing those lengths is itself a register choice, which a terminator symbol makes explicit. This is enough to bound the form of Eq. (5):

Theorem 4 (Symbol cost).

The Geometry of Refinement

A supremely helpful perspective for understanding this result is the geometric one. To do so we will establish a correspondence to the Hilbert curve [42]. This curve is constructed by first dividing a square into four quadrants, and drawing a curve that visits each quadrant in a specified order. This division can be repeated, repeating the curve on each quadrant up to a rotation that joins each curve to its neighbours. After kk such divisions the curve passes through each of the 4k4^k cells of a 2k×2k2^k\times2^k grid, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a).

This path is the order-kk Hilbert curve, and as k→∞k\to\infty it converges to a continuous curve through every point of the square. This is the standard example of a space-filling curve, with the useful property that cells that are close along the path remain close in space. Such curves are often used to order data in spatial databases and large matrix computations [43] for precisely this reason.

Already we see that the construction of the Hilbert curve echoes the self-similarity used to construct our representational cost functions. The curve passes through each cell of the square in order, and we may identify this with the rank. Each quadrant is filled in order, so the first 4k−14^{k-1} ranks occupy the first quadrant, the next 4k−14^{k-1} the second, and so forth. This holds inside every quadrant at every depth. A rank therefore names a quadrant, then a quadrant within that quadrant, and so on down to a single cell. Just as for registers and symbols, each square in the construction is a register for the cells it contains, while being itself one of the four symbols of the register above it. Returning to Eq. (6), we see that it holds at every depth without reordering a single rank, which is why the sixteen symbols of Fig. 2 could be regrouped along one path. Refinement is just as uniform. Passing from 4k4^k to 4k+14^{k+1} distinctions repeats the motif in each of the 4k4^k registers at depth kk.

If we assign a price to each repetition of the motif, it must be recursively self-consistent by token of the fact that every repetition acts on a smaller square. Each square register/symbol is parametrised only by its width wi=2−iw_i=2^{-i} in units of the original square, and there are wi−2=4iw_i^{-2}=4^i of them. Any cost we might set on iterating the curve must therefore be in proportion to wγw^{\gamma}. For example, with γ=0\gamma=0 the cost of division is constant, for γ=1\gamma=1 it scales with length and for a resource spread through its area γ=2\gamma=2. Iterating the curve from m=4km=4^k cells to mnmn (n=4jn=4^j) then takes the curve from order kk to k+j−1k+j-1. Since 4iwiγ=4iα4^i w_i^{\gamma}=4^{i\alpha} with α=1−γ/2\alpha=1-\gamma/2, we may express the differential cost of refinement of Eq. (13) as

Δm(n)=Δ1(4)∑i=kk+j−14iwiγ=mαΔ1(n),\Delta_m(n) =\Delta_1(4)\sum_{i=k}^{k+j-1}4^{i}w_i^{\gamma} =m^{\alpha}\,\Delta_1(n), (35)

Defining C0=Δ1(4)C_0=\Delta_1(4) for the price of the first division, after some tedious algebra we may establish contact with Eq. (15):

ϵ(m,n)=(mα−1)Δ1(n),\epsilon(m,n)=\bigl(m^\alpha-1\bigr)\Delta_1(n), (36)

where

Δ1(n)={C0(nα−1)/(4α−1),α≠0,C0logn/log4α=0.\Delta_1(n) = \begin{cases} C_0(n^\alpha-1)/(4^\alpha-1), &\alpha\ne0,\\[1ex] C_0\log n/\log4 &\alpha=0. \end{cases} (37)

The complete cost function is then specified by Eqs. (32) and (33) with κα=αC0/(4α−1)\kappa_\alpha=\alpha C_0/(4^\alpha-1) for α≠0\alpha \neq 0 and κ0=C0/log4\kappa_0=C_0/\log4.

At this point a natural generalisation suggests itself. The Hilbert curve takes place in a dimension d=2d=2 and each iteration multiplies distinctions by b=4b=4, but we can imagine a more general geometry in which both the dimensionality and refinement can take a range of values. Let each iteration divide every cell into bb congruent copies of itself, just as the square divides into its quadrants. What a division shares among the copies is the volume of their parent, and it is volume that keeps count of the distinctions. At depth ii there are bib^i cells, each holding a fraction b−ib^{-i} of the register’s volume, so the distinctions resolved at that depth are counted by the inverse of a cell’s share. Width is then a derived quantity, wi=b−i/dw_i=b^{-i/d}, which returns wi=2−iw_i=2^{-i} for the square. A resource of dimension γ\gamma costs each cell in proportion to wiγw_i^\gamma, and the divisions at depth ii together cost Ci=C0biwiγ=C0biαC_i=C_0\,b^i w_i^{\gamma}=C_0\,b^{i\alpha}, where

α=1−γd.\alpha=1-\frac{\gamma}{d}. (38)

The refinement prices of Eqs. (35) and (36) carry over with 44 replaced by bb, so this exponent fixes the entire cost function. Note that bb has dropped out of it entirely. Whether the price of refinement grows, holds steady or dies away is decided by a single ratio, the dimension of the resource a division consumes against the dimension of the space it divides.

The logarithm of Theorem 2 now acquires a simple geometric meaning. Homogeneous refinement, α=0\alpha=0, requires γ=d\gamma=d, so that a division is paid for in proportion to the volume it divides. Volume is exactly what subdivision conserves. However many cells a level contains, and whatever their shape, together they fill the same register, and dividing all of them costs what it cost to divide the whole once. This is the geometric content of distinctions that multiply while their costs add. A resource of lower dimension is instead shared among cells whose number grows faster than each one’s share shrinks, and the price of every new level rises with the range as NαN^\alpha. Nothing about the material has entered yet - only the dimension of the resource and that of the space.

Two of our earlier results now meet in the dimension of the resource. A resource contained within a dd-dimensional cell cannot have dimension greater than dd, so α≥0\alpha\ge0, and the negative exponents we have already excluded as unphysical are precisely those that would require it. In the other direction, Theorem 3 caps every efficient budget at Kreg(b)⌈logbN⌉K_{\mathrm{reg}}(b)\lceil\log_b N\rceil. A register refined at a power-law price can certainly be built, but as its range grows, independent registers composed positionally will eventually do the same job for less. Containment bounds γ\gamma from above while efficiency bounds it from below, and the two meet only at γ=d\gamma=d. An efficient register pays for each division in proportion to the volume it divides.

This is harder to satisfy than it sounds, because the natural way to keep one distinction apart from another is a wall. If each register needs its own perimeter, and that perimeter is smooth, then γ=d−1\gamma=d-1 and α=1/d\alpha=1/d. For the square, every level then needs twice as much wall as the one before it. A wall can meet the demand only by ceasing to be smooth. Self-similar tiles of the plane are generically rough in exactly this sense, the square being the exception rather than the rule [47]. A perimeter of dimension D∂D_\partial between d−1d-1 and dd gives α=1−D∂/d\alpha=1-D_\partial/d, and approaches homogeneous refinement as it approaches filling the space it divides. Figure 4 compares four tiles of the plane, each dividing into bb congruent copies of itself and each ordered by a space-filling curve that generalises the Hilbert path. Priced by area they are indistinguishable, since every level divides the same area once. Priced by their perimeters they separate by the dimension of their boundaries alone [Fig. 4(e,f)]. The Lévy dragon, whose boundary very nearly fills the plane, is almost logarithmic, while the terdragon - which divides in three, as radix economy favours - owes its place to the roughness of its edges rather than to its radix. The comparison suggests a principle we might call fractal encoding: wherever distinctions must be held apart by walls, an efficient register should draw those walls as close to filling space as its material allows. Its boundaries then acquire the economy of a volume, and each further digit of an address costs nearly what the last one did.

The perimeter also leaves a trace that can be read without weighing it. Perturb a point placed uniformly within a register by a short isotropic step, and read its address before and after. For short steps the chance of crossing a wall is proportional to its length, so the address at depth kk changes with a probability proportional to the total perimeter CkC_k of the cells at that depth. Every wall at depth kk is also a wall at depth k+1k+1, so a step that changes the coarser address changes the finer one too. Of the steps that change a point’s address at depth k+1k+1, the fraction P(k|k+1)P(k\,|\,k+1) that also change it at depth kk is therefore

P(k|k+1)=CkCk+1=b−α.P(k\,|\,k+1)=\frac{C_k}{C_{k+1}}=b^{-\alpha}. (39)

How often a small perturbation changes the coarser address measures the exponent of the refinement law, without any material being counted. In the Gosper hierarchy three of the seven children of a parent have their neighbour in any given direction outside it, so P(k|k+1)=3/7P(k\,|\,k+1)=3/7 at every depth and α=1−ln3/ln7=1−D∂/2\alpha=1-\ln3/\ln7=1-D_\partial/2. Figure 5 shows the readout on the hexagonal lattice that underlies the Gosper island, where Eq. (39) holds exactly. The identity itself needs no self-similarity, and Appendix E confirms it on irregular partitions as well. What self-similarity adds is that the readout returns the same exponent at every scale.

The demand of Eq. (3) carries no length scale - it is the same at every depth of the hierarchy. The matter that meets it does carry one. A rough wall is built from elements of finite size aa, and it can only be rough between that scale and the size of the register it encloses, since tracing an outline of span ww requires of order (w/a)D∂(w/a)^{D_\partial} elements only while w≫aw\gg a. Refinement in place therefore ends where the cells reach the size of the elements that bound them. At that scale a register can no longer be divided, only combined with others, and this is where the boundary of independence assumed in Eq. (26) is physically drawn. The self-similar account of a register’s interior terminates at its smallest unit, and beyond it further range is bought by positional composition at the logarithmic price of Theorem 3.

The same contest between bulk and boundary runs well beyond registers. Entanglement in the ground state of a gapped local Hamiltonian grows with the boundary of a region, where that of a generic state grows with its volume [48]. At criticality the entropy of a block of LL sites grows as (c/3)lnL(c/3)\ln L, with cc the central charge [49], and entanglement renormalisation traces this logarithm to every scale contributing equally [50], just as every level of a homogeneous register adds the same cost. In Murray’s arterial tree every symmetric fork repeats the same radius ratio , and such networks fill the space they serve before terminating in units of fixed size, such as capillaries and leaves [52]. In each case a demand without a scale is met by matter with one, and the resource each division consumes decides the shape. What none of these examples fixes is the price C0C_0 of a single division, which depends on the operation performed. If that operation is the reversible isothermal reset of a uniformly occupied bb-state memory, then C0=kBTlogbC_0=k_BT\log b [20], and the rate κ0=C0/logb\kappa_0=C_0/\log b is simply kBTk_BT. A memory in use is rarely uniformly occupied, however. To find what its reset actually costs, we must follow a machine whose register is filled by its own guesses.

IV. The Guessing Machine

The preceding section priced a representation by its organisation. But what does a representation physically do with the distinctions it holds? Sooner or later it must forget them, and Landauer showed that forgetting has a price: returning a memory to a standard state dissipates heat [18]. To see what that price is for a representation in use, consider a machine that proposes an answer, checks it, and starts again when the answer is wrong. Its register must hold a candidate long enough to test it, and to reuse the register the machine must decide what to keep and what to discard. The abstract distinctions of the previous sections have become something the apparatus actually does.

Why choose such an unassuming machine? It exposes the three jobs we want to separate: generating a candidate, verifying it, and clearing the space for another attempt. A deep result in mathematical logic lets us express a broad class of computations in this language. The same result has already carried questions about physical systems into the theory of computation, showing that whether a quantum system can be steered to a chosen target is in general undecidable [53]. Here it lets one concrete machine stand for computation in general, and the erasure bound then attaches a physical price to the uncertainty its protocol discards.

Computation as Diophantine search

Call a set recursively enumerable if there exists a systematic procedure - an algorithm - that can confirm membership of any element in the set, though it may run indefinitely on non-members [54]. This includes every set for which a positive answer can be confirmed by a computation that terminates. The Davis–Putnam–Robinson–Matiyasevich (DPRM) theorem states that every such set 𝒮⊆ℕm\mathcal S\subseteq\mathbb N^m can be characterised by integer solutions to a Diophantine equation, a polynomial equation D(u,x)D(u,x)

u∈𝒮⇔∃x∈ℕn:D(u,x)=0,u\in\mathcal S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists x\in\mathbb N^n:D(u,x)=0, (40)

with integer coefficients [55, 56]. Here uu is a tuple of parameters, the input to the computation, and xx is a tuple of witnesses. A witness is a candidate whose existence certifies a positive answer, much as an explicit counterexample certifies a conjecture false. Once a witness is supplied, verification reduces to evaluating D(u,x)D(u,x) and testing whether it vanishes.

The distinction between finding a witness and checking one is the familiar point. The theorem gives a finite verification procedure once the candidate is in hand, not a general bound on the resources required to find it. Its reach is nevertheless striking: every computably confirmable positive answer has such a polynomial witness relation. An integer polynomial can thus stand in for a computation without resembling the program that performs it.

The polynomial encodes the logical relation, not its implementation: whether that is realised by logic gates, biochemical pathways or neural wiring, the relation is still described by Eq. (40). Nothing obliges the device to guess at random, and the translation need not preserve its time, memory or heat costs. Guessing and checking witnesses are nevertheless the most useful terms in which to make the physical bookkeeping explicit, and we now use them to construct an example protocol.

Guess, test, erase

For fixed input uu, let pup_u be a distribution over the candidate witnesses. The machine repeats the following cycle:

1. Guess. Sample a candidate x∼pux\sim p_u and hold it in the witness register.

2. Test. Evaluate D(u,x)D(u,x) to determine whether the candidate satisfies the constraint.

3. Erase. If the test fails, reset the witness register to a fixed reference state and try again. If it succeeds, retain the answer and halt.

The machine is deliberately unimaginative. Its purpose is to make the physical jobs visible, not to recommend an efficient search algorithm. Independent guesses find a solution almost surely whenever the satisfying witnesses have positive total probability. Exhaustive enumeration is another possible strategy. Improving the guess distribution changes how often the machine succeeds, while changing what it retains changes the information eventually sent to reset.

This also makes the connection to Section II concrete. The physical memory holding the candidate is a register RR, its readouts are the witness symbols xx, and the test specifies which candidates answer the input uu. A witness must be represented in matter before it can be checked, and Fig. 6 separates that representation from the test and the reset that follows. Appendix F gives the formal setup and discusses witness encodings.

The cost of a reset

What, then, does the machine pay when it throws away a wrong answer? After a failed test, the register must be made available for the next candidate. The test has told us that the answer was wrong, but it has not generally told us which wrong answer was stored. It is this remaining uncertainty that a reset removes. A register therefore has three distinct attributes: its capacity, the ensemble occupying it, and the protocol used to reset it.

Let p(x)p(x) be the ensemble of logical states entering a complete classical reset. We take a bath initially thermal at T>0T>0 and uncorrelated with the memory, and a protocol that uses no retained side information. With the internal entropy of a logical state restored by the cycle, the memory entropy decreases by kBH(p)k_BH(p). Landauer’s bound is then

⟨Qerase⟩≥kBTH(p),H(p)=−∑xp(x)lnp(x).\langle Q_{\mathrm{erase}}\rangle\ge k_BT H(p), \qquad H(p)=-\sum_xp(x)\ln p(x). (41)

This is the entropy-decrease form of the principle [18, 20]. Picture the register as a particle that may sit in any of its cells. Resetting it means compressing the particle into the reference cell whichever cell it occupied, much as a piston compresses a one-molecule gas, and the work this compression requires leaves as heat. What is discarded is which cell the particle was in, and its uncertainty is measured by H(p)H(p) rather than by the size of the numeral written in memory.

A witness known to equal a large integer MM has zero logical entropy, however many digits are used to display it. Conversely, a register of capacity NN has entropy lnN\ln N only when its NN states are equally likely. This is why the occupied ensemble must be specified before a register budget is turned into an erasure cost - capacity tells us what the register can distinguish, while the distribution tells us which uncertainty this reset removes.

The typicality ordering introduced in Section II now acquires a physical interpretation. We order the states by how likely they are to enter the reset. Relabelling a witness cannot change this order or the uncertainty removed, even though it can change the integer used to name it. Probability rank therefore supplies the invariant we need. List the positive probabilities in decreasing order, p1≥p2≥⋯p_1\ge p_2\ge\cdots, breaking ties consistently, and define the mean log-rank

ℓ(p):=∑r≥1prlnr.\ell(p):=\sum_{r\ge1}p_r\ln r. (42)

We call this the rank surprisal. The first rr probabilities are each at least prp_r, so

rpr≤∑j=1rpj≤1,−lnpr≥lnr.rp_r\le\sum_{j=1}^r p_j\le1,\qquad -\ln p_r\ge\ln r. (43)

Averaging and applying Eq. (41) gives

⟨Qerase⟩≥kBTH(p)≥kBTℓ(p).\boxed{\langle Q_{\mathrm{erase}}\rangle \ge k_BT H(p)\ge k_BT\ell(p).} (44)

The physical content of the bound is now attached to rank itself. A state at probability rank rr cannot have probability greater than 1/r1/r, so its surprisal cannot be smaller than lnr\ln r. Averaging converts this elementary ordering fact into a lower bound on the uncertainty erased and hence on the mean heat. We need neither the numerical size of a witness nor a particular memory architecture to make that comparison, only the order of the bars in Fig. 6. This extends the use of rank from pricing a vocabulary to pricing a specified operation on its occupied states. The distribution must be the one actually erased, under the reset assumptions above. With that ensemble fixed, changing the labels of its states cannot change the bound. For countable ensembles the entropy comparison holds as an inequality of non-negative extended sums. Finite entropy makes all the information quantities finite.

The rank of the known witness MM is one, as it should be. For a uniform ensemble, ℓ=ln(N!)/N=lnN−1+o(1)\ell=\ln(N!)/N=\ln N-1+o(1), so rank surprisal follows the entropy to leading order. Uniformly refining each state into nn sub-states adds lnn\ln n nats independently of the original range. In the reversible isothermal limit its additional reset cost is kBTlnnk_BT\ln n. This provides a physical realisation of homogeneous refinement for a uniform-reset budget. The relevant order in Eq. (44) is always that of the ensemble actually erased. Appendix G develops the uniform-reset model when refinement prices vary with depth.

For the guessing cycle, the relevant ensemble is the one that reaches the reset after failure. Successful witnesses have left the cycle, so they must not be included as though they too were erased at that step.

Let FF denote failure and ηu=Pr(F∣u)\eta_u=\Pr(F\mid u). The ensemble entering reset is puE(x)=Pr(x∣F,u)p^E_u(x)=\Pr(x\mid F,u), rather than the unconditioned guess distribution. The witness-reset contribution per attempt therefore obeys

⟨Qwitness⟩attempt≥ηukBTH(puE)≥ηukBTℓ(puE).\langle Q_{\mathrm{witness}}\rangle_{\mathrm{attempt}} \ge\eta_u k_BT H(p^E_u) \ge\eta_u k_BT\ell(p^E_u). (45)

Testing, control records and resetting a successful output contribute their own costs. If retained classical information ZZ is used during reset, the corresponding bound uses H(X∣Z)H(X\mid Z), and the rank comparison applies within each conditional ensemble. Reversible computation can postpone erasure by keeping this information [57, 20].

A failed guess has not become physically free merely because it was wrong. The machine may keep a record of it - in which case that record occupies another register - or discard it and pay the reset cost of the uncertainty removed. Keeping the record can make future choices better informed, but whether that benefit repays its storage and access costs is a question about the organisation of the whole process. The problem recurs one level up - it is self-similar, just as the registers of Section III were.

The same logical computation can consequently have different physical costs depending on what is retained and when it is reset. The guessing machine gives a concrete operation to which our rank description can be applied. For a fixed erased ensemble, probability rank survives an invertible relabelling of the witnesses, while the detailed apparatus fixes the additional costs. We can now turn the question around: if the mean logarithmic rank cost is specified but the occupation probabilities are not, what distribution should we infer?

V. The Canonical Cost Curve

So far we have asked what it costs to support distinctions and what is paid when uncertainty over them is erased. A register’s use also has statistics: some symbols are encountered often, while others are rare. The cost law does not by itself specify those frequencies. We must distinguish the physical constraint from the inference we make when that constraint is all we know.

We use the canonical symbol cost K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r, selected by homogeneous refinement and the prefix-budget idealisation of Eq. (31). Physical use induces a distribution p(r)p(r) over these states, with mean cost

ℓ=∑rp(r)logr.\ell=\sum_r p(r)\log r. (46)

This is the rank surprisal, the expected logarithmic cost of the occupied symbol. Many distributions share the same mean. Choosing one of them therefore requires an inference rule. If the budget is all we know, how do we avoid supplying additional structure merely because we prefer it?

The rank ensemble

The answer is supplied by Jaynes’ principle of maximum entropy [58]. Among distributions consistent with the stated constraints, it chooses the one that makes the least additional commitment relative to the specified state space and reference measure. This is the sense in which maximum entropy is a requirement of honest inference. The consistency requirements studied by Shore and Johnson provide a related axiomatic basis [59].

Here the reference is counting measure on the ranks: before conditioning on cost, each available rank receives the same weight. A different density of available states would instead enter as a prior weight. With this choice made, we maximise the Shannon entropy of a vocabulary of size RR,

S[p]=−∑r=1Rp(r)logp(r)S[p]=-\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log p(r) (47)

under

∑r=1Rp(r)=1,∑r=1Rp(r)logr=ℓ.\sum_{r=1}^R p(r)=1,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^R p(r)\log r=\ell. (48)

The multiplier λ\lambda conjugate to mean cost prices departures towards more expensive ranks. The maximum-entropy weight of a state is exponential in its cost. Since that cost is logarithmic, e−λlogr=r−λe^{-\lambda\log r}=r^{-\lambda}: the exponential in cost becomes a power law in rank. Normalisation gives

p(r∣λ)=r−λZR(λ),p(r\mid\lambda)=\frac{r^{-\lambda}}{Z_R(\lambda)}, (49)

where

ZR(λ)=∑r=1Rr−λ.Z_R(\lambda)=\sum_{r=1}^R r^{-\lambda}. (50)

Theorem 5 (Zipf equilibrium).

Let R≥2R\ge2 and K(r)=logrK(r)=\log r on {1,…,R}\{1,\dots,R\}. For 0<ℓ<logR0<\ell<\log R, the maximum-entropy distribution at fixed mean cost ℓ\ell is p(r∣λ)=r−λ/ZR(λ)p(r\mid\lambda)=r^{-\lambda}/Z_R(\lambda). The equation of state ℓ(λ)=−∂λlogZR(λ)\ell(\lambda)=-\partial_\lambda\log Z_R(\lambda) uniquely determines finite λ\lambda. The endpoint ensembles are obtained as λ→±∞\lambda\to\pm\infty.

The theorem says that a power law need not be supplied as a further guess about the occupation statistics. It is the canonical maximum-entropy weight of a logarithmically priced rank. The calculation, together with its extension to scale-covariant costs, is given in Appendix H. For rank to agree with decreasing occupation probability we use λ≥0\lambda\ge0, with λ=0\lambda=0 giving uniform occupation.

Mandelbrot obtained related power laws by assigning logarithmic word costs [60]. The preceding register and symbol theorems explain where the leading rate of that cost can come from once the organisation is itself included. The canonical model goes further by taking the logarithmic symbol law to be exact. Under that cost and the stated mean constraint, the occupation law is then fixed rather than fitted independently. This is the sequence of the argument: composition constrains the cost, and maximum entropy turns that cost into a distribution. Moreover, this rank law is not tied to a single mechanism. Power law distributions have been derived in a variety of previous contexts [61, 62]. Our contribution is to establish the minimal systemic properties under which these Zipfian distributions obtain. The fact that these describe so many biological and societal phenomena is extremely suggestive. Consider for example that word frequencies in many languages exhibit a power law distribution with exponents close to λ=1\lambda=1. From this we may infer two facts. First, that natural language is close to an optimal representation under additive composition [Footnote 4. One could argue that language creation is a form of subadditive composition. No cap.] . Secondly, its exponent tells us that we humans and bots are prepared to pay an enormous cost for linguistic facility. Understanding why this last statement must be true is our next concern.

The cost curve

What does this distribution allow us to calculate? The partition sum is more than a normalising constant. Throughout, we have priced a representation by its cost alone. What makes a representation useful rather than merely cheap is what it preserves about a target, the question posed by the information bottleneck [22] and, in a different guise, by the free-energy principle [64]. A static account of cost cannot settle it. Once the framework is extended to dynamics, however, whatever the evolution of a representation is steered towards must specify the target that the representation serves.

VI. Outlook

Every representation has to be built out of something, but this does not fix how its constituents must be organised. Representation can change without changing the target it represents. The sixteen symbols of Fig. 2 make this freedom explicit: a smaller choice within a register must be paid for by a larger choice of register. Once both are included, the problem repeats at the level of the apparatus itself. This is the central purpose of the recursive account developed here, to give the organisation of a representation a cost that can be compared with the cost of its contents.

That account has produced three results. The first is the form of the cost itself, which efficiency and recursive self-consistency alone fix. A useful way to view the logarithmic result is through the freedom to change coordinates. A monolithic register may be expensive to enlarge, yet its components can support the same distinctions more economically when used as digits. The asymptotic price is set by the infimum of the costs per nat available to that construction. The register theorem supplies both a ceiling from every available digit and a common limiting rate. The symbol theorem then shows that paying for selection and termination need not increase the infimum of leading rates. The generalisation is therefore not just a logarithm assigned to another kind of symbol. It is a comparison of physical organisations that prices the machinery needed to make those symbols accessible.

The second result is a correspondence with geometry. Before any digits are composed, a single register can only be refined, and the geometric examples show what that involves: the number of subdivisions grows while the resource consumed by each subdivision shrinks. Their balance fixes the refinement law. The logarithm is the case in which every multiplicative increase of range has the same price, reached when the resource consumed by each division fills the space it divides. Self-similar tiles ordered by space-filling curves realise a range of these laws, set by the dimension of their boundaries, and the exponent can be read from how often a small perturbation changes an address. On this account, the logarithm is the cost of counting with physical registers.

The third result is a thermodynamic realisation. Through Diophantine search, the guessing machine of Section IV stands for computation in general, and the heat it dissipates in forgetting its guesses is bounded below by the mean logarithm of their probability rank. Together these results suggest why the same logarithm recurs across so many sciences. Information theory prices a message by the length of its code, thermodynamics prices a reset by the entropy it removes, and Zipf’s law finds the frequency of a word falling as a power of its rank. Each describes distinctions that must be held by something, and each inherits its logarithm from the efficient embodiment of those distinctions rather than from the particulars of its own substrate. The account developed here replaces none of these theories. It offers instead a foundation for a covering theory across them, in which the organisation of a representation is itself a physical object of optimisation.

The logarithm of rank also gives the Zipf boundary a concrete meaning. In the finite harmonic distribution, each octave of rank carries approximately the same probability. The vocabulary uses many scales without selecting one of them as special. A direct physical example is the harmonic entanglement catalyst [65], whose ordered Schmidt probabilities are

pr=1rHR,HR=∑j=1R1j.p_r=\frac{1}{rH_R},\qquad H_R=\sum_{j=1}^R\frac1j. (59)

Its organisation across scales enables catalytic transformations. The finite cutoff is part of that construction, just as it is part of a physical register. In the classical reset model, enlarging this harmonic vocabulary raises the entropy removed per complete reset as 12lnR+lnlnR+O(1)\frac12\ln R+\ln\ln R+O(1).

The question of useful complexity is equally concrete in physical learning. Learning capacity has been repeatedly linked to dynamic phases [66, 67], while the response of an accessible subsystem is linked to spectral nonflatness and metrological sensitivity [68]. In these systems, generating more structure is useful only insofar as the available readout can use it. This gives the extension of the cost account to targets a practical setting: compare the information preserved about the same task, the organisation needed to access it, and the resources consumed by preparing, reading and resetting that organisation. Furthermore, it emphasises the view of computation as a form of dynamics.

Where does this leave learning? Representation learning already makes such choices by finding variables in which useful structure becomes accessible [69]. Architecture search [70] and pruning [4, 5] extend the choice to the apparatus holding those variables. The present theory supplies a static comparison between the resulting organisations. A dynamical theory must then price the changes between them, including the information retained along the way. This is where the nearly decomposable hierarchy [71] becomes a physical design question: when does the saving within a component repay the cost of selecting and coordinating it?

The question is sharpest for a representation that is part of its own target. A learner that improves its own organisation must also represent that organisation, and part of what it must represent is how to represent itself more efficiently. Automated research and recursive self-improvement make this reflexive case a practical one. The procedure that proposes a change can itself be rewritten [6, 7, 8]. Its evaluator, archive and memory belong to the same physical account. Keeping unsuccessful candidates can make later searches more informative, but requires storage and access. Discarding them releases capacity while erasing uncertainty about the history, at the price the guessing machine makes explicit. The trade-off concerns the entire process by which improvement is achieved. Connecting these static budgets to learning rates will require the duration and dissipation of the allowed operations [32, 23]. With those ingredients, whether such costs bound the rate of self-improvement becomes a well-posed physical question. Answering it is the task of a thermodynamic theory of self-improvement, which we take up in a sequel.

The cost of complexity is therefore also a question of where complexity is placed. The same distinctions can be prohibitively expensive in one organisation and accessible in another. Any universal theory of learning must account for that freedom, including when the learner turns it on itself. When it comes to accounting, the universe makes no special exceptions. Finally, more recursively minded readers may have noticed that this theory of representation applies even to itself, and to the best of our knowledge remains consistent with itself. Just imagine what it cost to organise.
