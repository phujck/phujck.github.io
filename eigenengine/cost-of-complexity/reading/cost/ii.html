The Cost of Complexity: A Physical Theory of Representation · Gerard McCaul

II. Representations in Matter

Our starting point is deliberately sparse. A representation must be realised in some physical system, and its use draws on the resources of that system. This applies to the apparatus that organises the representation as much as to the symbols it holds [Footnote 1. There are no exceptions. It applies just as much to the theory proposed here as to any domain of application. This makes everything that follows unavoidably self-referential, and more than a bit meta.] . Thermodynamic accounts of computation make these resources explicit through the operations performed, their duration and the states available to implement them [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. We therefore seek a description of structural organisation before choosing a particular substrate.

To explore the consequences of embodiment, we must first establish what is being represented and what carries the representation. Let Ω\Omega be the space of microscopic configurations relevant to the problem, and let y:Ω→Yy:\Omega\to Y specify the target distinctions. The target need not be a complete microscopic description. It might ask which of sixteen intervals contains an instrument reading, without asking for the positions of every atom in the instrument.

We call a physical system a register when its distinguishable states are used to realise a set of alternatives. The term is not restricted to computer memory. The position of a pointer, the level of an electrical signal, or a pattern across several physical elements can all serve this purpose. A register may therefore be a single device or an organised collection of devices. Its range is the number of alternatives it can distinguish at the resolution required by the task.

Write RR for the physical register and XX for its set of symbols. These play different roles: RR is the apparatus, while x∈Xx\in X names one of the alternatives realised by that apparatus. Sixteen symbols need not require sixteen separate objects. They could be sixteen positions of one pointer or sixteen joint configurations of several elements. The readout specifies which physical configurations count as the same symbol. We express this through the symbol map

π:Ω→X.\pi:\Omega\longrightarrow X. (1)

The configurations in Ax=π−1(x)A_x=\pi^{-1}(x) all realise the same symbol. Microscopic changes within AxA_x leave the readout unchanged, so the symbol map treats those configurations as indistinguishable. This is the coarse-graining supplied by the representation. What matters is whether the distinctions it removes are needed for the target.

The representation is faithful to the target when π(ω)=π(ω′)\pi(\omega)=\pi(\omega') implies y(ω)=y(ω′)y(\omega)=y(\omega'). It may then discard microscopic detail while retaining everything the target requires. For a probabilistic target the corresponding condition is that its conditional distribution depend on ω\omega only through π(ω)\pi(\omega), the familiar sufficiency condition [33]. When the target is the distribution of future observations, this leads to the causal-state construction of computational mechanics [10].

Critically, the target may include the register itself. This is the situation faced by a system that represents its own organisation, and it requires the apparatus to remain inside the resource account. Exact fidelity is still a question about the chosen target: a one-bit register can preserve a binary property of a much larger system. When the target demands more distinctions than the available register can hold, the representation must choose what to retain.

Which distinctions can a physical device infer in the first place? This is answered within the inference-device framework, which treats observation, prediction and recollection within a common formal account [34]. We therefore take the target as given and ask what it costs to support the distinctions needed to represent it.

The price of embodiment

A symbol map tells us which distinction is represented, but not what must be provided to realise it. We therefore assign a realisation cost Kℛ(x)K_{\mathcal R}(x) to symbol xx in an organisation ℛ\mathcal R. Here ℛ\mathcal R specifies the arrangement of registers, the assignment of symbols to their states, and the means of selecting among them. A particular register RR is one component of that organisation. Changing ℛ\mathcal R can change the cost of reaching xx without changing what xx represents.

All comparisons use the same resource, operating conditions and task. Depending on the application, the budget may price apparatus, a prescribed operation, or a declared combination of them. Providing a register with sixteen possible states and repeatedly resetting one of those states are different resource questions. Experimental erasure and finite-time information processing illustrate why the operation must be specified when the budget is given an energetic meaning [35, 36, 37]. Under the standard reset assumptions, erasing an unbiased bit at temperature TT has a mean heat floor kBTln2k_BT\ln2. The general Landauer bound prices the entropy removed [18, 19, 20]. Section IV will use this operation to calibrate a logarithmic cost in energy units. For the time being however we will proceed with maximum generality, and use only the fact that physical manipulations are never free.

What makes one allocation of these costs more efficient than another? A representation is more than a set of labels. Its symbols stand in correspondence with the observations or alternatives they describe, and some may be encountered more readily than others. We begin with only this relative information, rather than a complete probability measure. Instead we shall rely only on the weaker notion of typicality,

x≽x′⇔x is at least as typical as x′.x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{$x$ is at least as typical as $x'$}. (2)

Typicality records which symbols are more prevalent or readily encountered. It says that one comes before another, without specifying how much more probable it is. If usage probabilities are known, they can supply this order, but the order alone does not determine them. We consider a finite vocabulary or a countable sequence in which every symbol has finitely many predecessors. For clarity we initially take distinct typicalities. The order can be represented by a scalar τ\tau, but changing its numerical scale leaves the ordering intact. Our argument must therefore depend on that order rather than on an arbitrary numerical encoding of it.

Our efficiency condition asks that cost respect this order:

x≽x′⇒Kℛ(x)≤Kℛ(x′).x\succcurlyeq x' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\mathcal R}(x)\le K_{\mathcal R}(x'). (3)

The motivation is the usual assignment argument. Suppose two physical states have different costs, and the symbol used more often has been assigned to the more expensive state. Exchanging their assignments reduces the mean cost. Repeating this comparison favours placing more probable symbols in cheaper states, whenever the physical implementation permits those exchanges [38, 39, 33].

Eq.(3) retains only the ordinal part of this argument. It permits equal costs and does not demand an optimal code. Nor does it specify how much a register costs when its range is enlarged or its contents are redistributed. Those questions concern the organisation of the representation. To answer them, we must include the cost of selecting a register alongside the cost of distinguishing its contents.
