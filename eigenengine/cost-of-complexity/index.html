The Cost of Complexity

A Physical Theory of Representation

Gerard McCaul · EigenEngine · 21 pages

A physical system can distinguish more things by putting them into groups and then choosing within a group. The paper asks what the apparatus that makes those choices costs. Its Hilbert example shows how one arrangement can have different costs depending on what each split consumes.

What does another distinction cost?

Start with a square. Divide it into four quarters, then divide each quarter into four again. You can now tell sixteen places apart. The paper asks what it costs to build the apparatus that lets you make those choices.

Choose a quarter, then a cell

Each quarter is a register, a physical system that holds a set of alternatives. Picking the quarter is itself a choice. The apparatus that makes it is a register too, so it belongs in the budget.

Cost to reach a cell = cost to provide and choose its quarter + cost to choose inside it

What adds up

Area pricing adds one unit every time the number of cells quadruples. The total grows as log N, where N is the number of cells.

Wall-length pricing adds twice as much at each round. The total grows as √N. Pricing by width cubed halves each new contribution and the total approaches two units.

When does the logarithm survive?

When the same multiplication of capacity costs the same at every size, the logarithm is exact. The paper calls that assumption homogeneous refinement.

The general bound needs a different assumption. If registers can be reused like digits and their complete costs add, a fixed-size digit repeated enough times sets a logarithmic upper bound on the cheapest design. A positive limiting price per unit of information gives logarithmic growth.

Erasing a failed guess releases heat

In a model that guesses, checks and resets after failure, the erased guesses form their own distribution. With a thermal bath at temperature T initially uncorrelated with the register, and a complete reset that uses no retained side information, Landauer’s principle puts a floor on the mean heat released. The paper bounds that floor using the guesses’ probability ranks.

⟨Q⟩ ≥ kBT H ≥ kBT ⟨log r⟩

Here H is the entropy of the guesses actually erased, and r puts the most likely erased guess first.

A logarithmic price gives a family of power laws

Take a finite vocabulary where the symbol in rank r costs log r to reach. Among distributions with the same mean cost, the one with greatest entropy is a power law.

p(r) ∝ r−λ

The mean cost sets λ. The case λ = 1 is Zipf’s law on a finite vocabulary. An infinite vocabulary needs λ > 1 to normalise.

Further results and evidence

The symbol-cost theorem concerns the cost of reaching a particular rank. A design needs to distinguish its entire more-typical prefix and pay for the selector and for identifying where each code ends. Under the paper’s conditions the asymptotic price per unit of information approaches the register rate.

The geometry can also change while the order stays fixed. Figure 4 puts the same hierarchy on self-similar sets of different dimensions. With wall-length pricing, the exponent decreases as the supporting set thins. In the disconnected cases the path just indicates rank order.

The paper also asks how much information a representation keeps about a chosen target. Its objective balances that fidelity against the cost of the register describing it.
